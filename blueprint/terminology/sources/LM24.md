## §A. What the paper defines

Each entry: **term / symbol** — defining quote (verbatim) — location — notes on variants, counts, conventions.

### A.0 Global objects and variables (§1.1, §1.2, §2.1, §3)

**A.0.1  $\mathbf{No}$, surreal numbers.** "Conway's ordered field $\mathbf{No}$ of surreal numbers [7] is a proper class containing the field $\mathbb{R}$ of real numbers as well as the class $\mathbf{On}$ of all ordinals equipped with Hessenberg's natural operations." — p. 2, §1.1. Formal definition in §2.3 (A.7.1). Always bold $\mathbf{No}$. Phrase used: "Conway's ordered field $\mathbf{No}$ of surreal numbers".

**A.0.2  Series representation of surreals; reverse well ordered.** "every surreal number can be expressed in a canonical way as a formal sum $\sum_{x\in\mathbf{No}} b_x \omega^x$, where each $b_x$ is in $\mathbb{R}$, and $\{x \in \mathbf{No} : b_x \neq 0\}$ is a set which is reverse well ordered, namely every non-empty subset has a maximum." — p. 2, §1.1. Convention: surreals are written as series in $\omega$ with *reverse* well ordered support (exponents increasing leftward); cf. A.0.9.

**A.0.3  $\mathbf{Oz}$, omnific integers.** "Inside $\mathbf{No}$, Conway isolated the subring $\mathbf{Oz}$ of the **omnific integers**: those are the surreal numbers $\sum_{x\in\mathbf{No}} b_x \omega^x$ where $b_0 \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $b_x = 0$ for all $x < 0$." — p. 2, §1.1. Always "omnific integer(s)" (never "omnific integers ring" or "surreal integers"); symbol always bold $\mathbf{Oz}$. Later identified as "$\mathbf{Oz} = \mathbb{Z} + \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}^{<0}))_{\mathbf{On}}$" (p. 75, Proof of Theorem C) and as "the special case in which $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbf{K} = \mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive group of $\mathbf{No}$ itself, and $\kappa = \mathbf{On}$" (p. 62, §8). The ring is referred to as "the ring $\mathbf{Oz}$ of omnific integers" (p. 61).

**A.0.4  integer part.** "$\mathbf{Oz}$ is an *integer part* of $\mathbf{No}$, namely for every $b \in \mathbf{No}$ there is a unique $c \in \mathbf{Oz}$ such that $c \leq b < c + 1$." — p. 2, §1.1. General form in Remark 2.1.2: "Let $\mathbf{K}$ be an ordered field and $\mathbf{Z}$ denote an *integer part* of $\mathbf{K}$, that is, $\mathbf{Z}$ is a subring of $\mathbf{K}$ such that for each $k \in \mathbf{K}$ there is a unique $\lfloor k \rfloor \in \mathbf{Z}$ such that $\lfloor k \rfloor \leq k < \lfloor k \rfloor + 1$." — p. 10. Notation $\lfloor k \rfloor$, $\lfloor b \rfloor$ for the integer-part map. (The notion is standard in the field; the paper defines it without citation, but cites [23], [16], [4] for results about it — see B.)

**A.0.5  refinement property.** "$\mathbf{Oz}$ has the **refinement property**: for all $b, c, d, e \in \mathbf{Oz}$, if $bc = de$, then there are $f, g, h, i \in \mathbf{Oz}$ such that $b = fg$, $c = hi$, $d = fh$, $e = gi$." — p. 3, Conjecture 1.1.1(2) (attributed to Conway [7, p46]). The paper treats this as the definition of the property; cf. B for "pre-Schreier".

**A.0.6  generalised power series; coefficients; exponents; support; $\operatorname{supp}(b)$; well-ordered.** "Given a field $K$ and a totally ordered abelian group $G$, a **generalised power series** with **coefficients** in $K$ and **exponents** in $G$ is a formal sum $b = \sum_{x\in G} b_x t^x$ with $b_x \in K$ such that the support $\operatorname{supp}(b) := \{x \in G : b_x \neq 0\}$ is well-ordered, namely every non-empty subset has a minimum." — p. 3, §1.2. Conventions fixed here: the formal variable is $t$; the coefficient of $t^x$ in $b$ is written $b_x$ (subscript, same letter); supports are well-ordered (minimum, not maximum); "well-ordered"/"well ordered" both appear (hyphen inconsistent in source). Throughout, the one-word noun is **series** (hundreds of occurrences: "a series", "the series $b$", "series with finite support"); "generalised power series" appears in the title, abstract, §1.2, §2.1 heading, §5.1 heading, and otherwise rarely. The paper never uses "Hahn series" as its own term except once in passing ("the theory of divisible ordered abelian groups and Hahn series", p. 18) and never "transseries". Variables for series: $b, c, d, e, p, q, r$ (lower-case; $b$ is the default series), never $f, g$ for series except in the refinement property; $p, q$ are reserved for series with finite support from §5 on.

**A.0.7  order type of a series; $\operatorname{ot}(b)$; the sequence form $\sum_{i<\alpha} b_i t^{x_i}$.** "We may also rewrite such sums as $\sum_{i<\alpha} b_i t^{x_i}$ where $\alpha$ is the unique ordinal isomorphic to $\operatorname{supp}(b)$ as a well ordered set, $(b_i : i < \alpha)$ is a sequence of non-zero elements of $K$, and $(x_i : i < \alpha)$ is a strictly increasing sequence in $G$. We call $\alpha$ the **order type** of $b$, denoted by $\operatorname{ot}(b)$." — p. 3, §1.2. Restated p. 12: "for $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, $\operatorname{supp}(b)$ is well ordered (by definition) and so we have $\operatorname{ot}(b) = \operatorname{ot}(\operatorname{supp}(b))$." Phrases: "the order type of $b$", "a series of order type $\omega \mathbin{\hat{+}} 2$", "has order type $\omega^2$", "of order types $\omega^2, \omega^3$". Always upright $\operatorname{ot}$.

**A.0.8  $K((G))$; $K((S))$.** "Let $K((G))$ denote the set of such series, which has a natural ring structure, and is in fact a field; and given a subset $S \subseteq G$, let $K((S))$ consist of the series with support contained in $S$." — p. 3, §1.2. Double-parenthesis notation for all-series rings; cf. single parenthesis A.1.9. Examples given: "$\mathbb{C}((\mathbb{Z})) = \mathbb{C}((t))$", "$\mathbb{C}((\mathbb{N})) = \mathbb{C}[[t]]$". Superscript-inequality notation for subsets of an ordered group is used without comment: $\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$, $\mathbb{R}^{<0}$, $G^{\leq 0}$, $G^{<0}$, $G^{\geq 0}$, $G^{>0}$, $\mathbf{No}^{\geq 0}$, $\mathbf{Oz}^{\geq 0}$, $H_\sigma^{<0}$, $\sigma^{<0}$ (for an Archimedean class $\sigma$, p. 65), $\mathbf{No}^{<b}$ (p. 19). The main ring is always written $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ / $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ — never "$K((\mathbb{R}_{\le 0}))$", never "$K((t^{\mathbb{R}^{\le0}}))$". Also "$K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) = K + K((\mathbb{R}^{<0}))$" (p. 3).

**A.0.9  Convention: $t$ versus $\omega^{-1}$.** "$\mathbf{No}$ can be seen as the union of the sets $\mathbb{R}((S))$ for $S$ subset of $\mathbf{No}$, thus as the class of series with coefficients in $\mathbb{R}$ and exponents in $\mathbf{No}$, after identifying $t$ with $\omega^{-1}$ (see § 2.3 for more details). For instance, $\omega = t^{-1}$, $1 + \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}} \omega^{1/(n+1)} = 1 + \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}} t^{-1/(n+1)}$. We will consistently use series in $\omega^{-1}$ to represent surreal numbers, while keeping $t$ for series in other fields $K((G))$." — p. 3, §1.2. Restated p. 17: "we shall write the series with $\omega$ in place of $t^{-1}$ to emphasise that we are talking about surreal numbers, as opposed to series in some $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$." Consequence: in $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ the standard examples have *negative* exponents, e.g. "$1 + \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}} t^{-1/(n+1)}$", "$t^{-\sqrt{2}} + t^{-1} + 1$"; in $\mathbf{Oz}$ the same objects are "$1 + \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}} \omega^{1/(n+1)}$", "$\omega^{\sqrt{2}} + \omega + 1$".

**A.0.10  Standing data of §3–§9 (bold = possibly proper class).** "From this point forward, we work with the following data: $\mathbf{K}$ a field of characteristic 0, possibly a proper class; $\mathbf{G}$ a divisible ordered abelian group, possibly a proper class; $\kappa$ an uncountable cardinal, or $\kappa = \mathbf{On}$." — p. 25, §3. Earlier: "Let $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{G}$ be also a field and a totally ordered abelian group, but whose underlying domains may be proper classes." — p. 9, §2.1. Convention: "the subscript $\kappa$ means that we restrict to those series whose support has size less than some uncountable cardinal $\kappa$, or whose support is a set when $\kappa = \mathbf{On}$" — p. 6, §1.4. The general ring is always written "$\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$" (with $\mathbf{Z}$ "a subring of $\mathbf{K}$", p. 6); the field-coefficient ring "$\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\leq 0}))_\kappa$"; the real-exponent ring "$\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$" (no $\kappa$, since $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})) = \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le0}))_{\mathbf{On}}$ and supports are countable, Remark 3.1.5). Sections 8–9: "$\mathbf{Z}$ a subring of $\mathbf{K}$, possibly a proper class" (p. 61).

**A.0.11  $\langle A \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$.** "Given a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ of real numbers, let $\langle A \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ denote the linear span of $A$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, seen as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space." — p. 4, §1.4. Generalised p. 21, §2.6: "Given a set $S \subseteq \mathbf{G}$, let $\langle S \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ denote its linear span over $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbf{G}$, seen as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space." Phrases: "$\langle \operatorname{supp}(c_i) \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has dimension $\geq 2$", "has dimension 1", "pairwise $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent" (said of supports).

**A.0.12  complete factorisation (informal).** "we provide a theory of *complete* factorisations in $\mathbf{Oz}$, in the sense that we express omnific integers as (possibly infinite) products of factors which are either irreducible, or reducible but with a well understood factorisation theory." — p. 4, §1.4. Informal; not used as a technical term later.

**A.0.13  $\operatorname{supp}(b) - v(b)$.** "where $\operatorname{supp}(b) - v(b)$ means $\operatorname{supp}(b) + (-v(b))$." — p. 21, §2.6.

### A.1 §2.1 Generalised power series

**A.1.1  Ring operations on $K((G))$.** "$\sum_{x\in G} b_x t^x + \sum_{x\in G} c_x t^x := \sum_{x\in G} (b_x + c_x) t^x$, $\left(\sum_{x\in G} b_x t^x\right) \left(\sum_{x\in G} c_x t^x\right) := \sum_{x\in G} \left(\sum_{y+z=x} b_y c_z\right) t^x$." — p. 9. Followed by: "One can easily verify that the right hand sides are well defined and in $K((G))$ (namely, the supports are well ordered, and for the second definition, each term $\sum_{y+z=x} b_y c_z$ contains at most finitely many non-zero summands)."

**A.1.2  monomial.** "Call **monomials** the series of the form $kt^x$ for $x \in G$ and $k \in K \setminus \{0\}$. Note that by the above definition, $t^x t^y = t^{x+y}$." — p. 9. Used constantly: "the monomials $t^x$", "divisible by $t^x$", "the only monomial divisors are elements of $\mathbf{K}$". Also "positive monomials" for surreals of the form $\omega^x$ (p. 76).

**A.1.3  Identification of $K$ with constants.** "With these operations, $K((G))$ is a $K$-algebra (for instance, the multiplicative identity is the monomial $t^0$). We identify the elements of $K$ with the monomials of the form $kt^0$." — p. 9.

**A.1.4  $K((G))_\kappa$; restricted; $\kappa$-bounded.** "let $K((G))_\kappa$ denote the subset of the series in $K((G))$ with support of cardinality strictly less than $\kappa$, where $\kappa$ is an uncountable cardinal; equivalently, $K((G))_\kappa$ is the union of $K((H))$ for $H$ ranging over the subgroups of $G$ of size less than $\kappa$. We call such series **restricted** or $\kappa$-**bounded**, and they form a $K$-algebra." — p. 9. Both adjectives offered; neither is used again in the body — the subscript notation $(\cdot)_\kappa$ dominates completely.

**A.1.5  $\mathbf{K}((G))$, $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_{\mathbf{On}}$, $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$ (class versions).** "We generalise the above to the class $\mathbf{K}$ by letting $\mathbf{K}((G))$ be the class $\bigcup_K K((G))$ for $K$ ranging over the subfields of $\mathbf{K}$ that are sets. For $\mathbf{G}$, we cannot give a general meaning to '$\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))$', as it involves the power of $\mathbf{G}$, but we may define $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_{\mathbf{On}}$ to be the class $\bigcup_G \mathbf{K}((G))$ for $G$ ranging over the subgroups of $\mathbf{G}$ that are sets." — pp. 9–10. "We may similarly define $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$, for $\kappa$ an uncountable cardinal, by restricting the above union to the subgroups of size less than $\kappa$. Note that $\mathbf{K}((G))_{\mathbf{On}} = \mathbf{K}((G))$." — p. 10. Convention: bare $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))$ with both bold is *never* written; the subscript $_{\mathbf{On}}$ or $_\kappa$ is mandatory for class-sized $\mathbf{G}$.

**A.1.6  algebraically closed; real closed (as used).** "(1) **algebraically closed** (namely, every non-trivial polynomial over $K((G))$ has a root in $K((G))$) if and only if $K$ is algebraically closed and $G$ is divisible; (2) **real closed** (namely, $K((G))$ becomes algebraically closed after, and only after, adjoining the square root of $-1$) if and only if $K$ is real closed and $G$ is divisible." — p. 10, Fact 2.1.1 (uncited).

**A.1.7  Hahn field; restricted / $\kappa$-bounded Hahn field.** "The field $K((G))$ is usually called a **Hahn field**, and $K((G))_\kappa$ is called restricted or $\kappa$-bounded Hahn field. We extend this terminology unchanged to respectively $\mathbf{K}((G))$ and $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$." — p. 10. Reported usage ("usually called"), no citation; recorded also in B.4. "Hahn field" appears again only at p. 60 ("the canonical valuation of the Hahn field $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{R})((\mathbf{G}))$").

**A.1.8  $K((S))$ for $S$ a subset / semigroup / monoid; $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{S}))_\kappa$.** "If $S$ is a subset of $G$, then $K((S))$ (the set of the series with support contained in $S$) is clearly a $K$-vector space. If $S$ is a semigroup, then $K((S))$ is closed under product; if $S$ is a monoid, then $K((S))$ also contains $t^0 = 1$ and so is a subring of $K((G))$. In particular, $K((G^{\leq 0}))$ is a subring of $K((G))$." — p. 10. "If $\mathbf{S}$ is a subclass of a class $\mathbf{G}$, then we can again define $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{S}))_\kappa$ as the union of $\mathbf{K}((S))$ for $S$ subset of $\mathbf{S}$ of size less than $\kappa$ (if $\kappa$ is a cardinal) or of any size (if $\kappa = \mathbf{On}$)." — p. 10.

**A.1.9  $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{S})$ — series with finite support (single parentheses).** "**Notation 2.1.5.** Given a subclass $\mathbf{S} \subseteq \mathbf{G}$, let $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{S})$ denote the subclass of $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{S}))_{\mathbf{On}}$ of the series with finite support." — p. 11. Followed by: "Just like for $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{S}))$, $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{S})$ is always a $\mathbf{K}$-vector space, and if $\mathbf{S}$ is a submonoid of $\mathbf{G}$, then $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{S})$ is a subring of $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{S}))_{\mathbf{On}}$." Heavily used: $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})$, $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G})$, $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G}^{\leq 0})$, $\mathbf{K}(H^{\le 0})$, $1 + \mathbf{K}(H^{<0})$, $\mathbb{N}(-\mathbf{On})$, $\mathbb{F}_p(\mathbb{Z})$, $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})$, $\mathrm{RV}_0(M)$ ("the group ring $\mathrm{RV}_0(M)$", p. 38). The paper's name for elements of $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le0})$ is "series with finite support" (dominant, dozens of times), occasionally "the subring of the series with degree at most 0" (p. 38), and in the $\mathbf{Oz}$ setting "pseudo-polynomial" (A.13). It never calls them "polynomials" or "generalised polynomials" in $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le0}))$, though it invokes the isomorphism with (Laurent) polynomial rings (Fact 2.1.6).

**A.1.10  truncation closed.** "$\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ is the unique integer part of $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$ containing $\mathbf{Z}$ that is *truncation closed*, that is, if it contains $\sum_x b_x t^x$, then it contains $\sum_{x<y} b_x t^x$ for all $y \in \mathbf{G}$." — p. 11, Remark 2.1.2. Also used for embeddings: "an embedding $\iota : R \to \mathbb{R}((G))$ with truncation closed image" (p. 11); "a truncation closed embedding into a Hahn group" (p. 73).

**A.1.11  $G_\infty$; conventions on $-\infty$; "the quirk $-\infty < -\infty$".** "Let $G_\infty := G \cup \{-\infty\}$. For $x \in G_\infty$, let $-\infty < x$, $-\infty + x = x + (-\infty) := -\infty$. Note the quirk $-\infty < -\infty$, which is convenient to avoid singling out the special value $-\infty$ in some statements." — p. 11. Same device for ordinals: "$\mathbf{On}_\infty := \mathbf{On} \cup \{-\infty\}$" (p. 13), for reals "$\mathbb{R}_\infty = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$" (p. 25), for monoids "$M_\infty = M \cup \{-\infty\}$" (p. 33). **Convention:** the valuation of $0$ is $-\infty$ (not $+\infty$); this is tied to the "multiplicative" orientation (A.0 / A.5.3).

**A.1.12  canonical ($t$-adic) valuation $v$.** "The **canonical** (or $t$-**adic**) valuation of $K((G))$ is the function $v : K((G)) \to G_\infty$ defined by $v(b) := \begin{cases} \min \operatorname{supp}(b) & b \neq 0 \\ -\infty & b = 0. \end{cases}$" — p. 11. Fact 2.1.3 lists its properties "(1) $v(b + c) \geq \min\{v(b), v(c)\}$; (2) $v(bc) = v(b) + v(c)$; (3) $v(b) = -\infty$ if and only if $b = 0$." Note the sign: $v$ is a *minimum* of the support with ultrametric inequality in the $\ge\min$ form, yet $v(0) = -\infty$ — a non-standard mix the paper adopts silently. "canonical valuation" dominates (pp. 9, 11, 60, 69: "canonical valuation $\prec v(b)$"); "$t$-adic" appears only in this definition. Symbol always plain $v$.

**A.1.13  units of $K((G^{\le 0}))$.** "**Corollary 2.1.4.** The units of $K((G^{\leq 0}))$, that is to say its invertible elements, are exactly the non-zero elements of $K$." — p. 11.

### A.2 §2.2 Ordinal arithmetic

**A.2.1  Set-theoretic frame.** "In order to work correctly with the proper classes $\mathbf{No}$, $\mathbf{On}$, our underlying theory will be the von Neumann-Bernays-Gödel set theory without choice (NBG). All the facts below are theorems of NBG." — p. 12. Later: "The axiom of global choice may be required when working with proper classes." — p. 72, Remark 8.4.5.

**A.2.2  ordinal; transitive; order type $\operatorname{ot}(A)$.** "A von Neumann **ordinal** is a set $\alpha$ such that the membership relation is a well order on $\alpha$ and that is also **transitive**, namely every element of $\alpha$ is an (initial) subset of $\alpha$. Every well ordered set $A$ is isomorphic to exactly one ordinal $\alpha$ as an ordered set, in which case we call $\alpha$ the **order type** of $A$ and write $\alpha = \operatorname{ot}(A)$." — p. 12.

**A.2.3  $\mathbf{On}$; $<$ on ordinals.** "Given two ordinals $\alpha, \beta$, say that $\alpha$ is **less than** $\beta$, written $\alpha < \beta$, if $\alpha \in \beta$ (in particular $\alpha \subsetneq \beta$, since ordinals are transitive). The (proper) class of all ordinals is denoted by $\mathbf{On}$, and $<$ is a well order on $\mathbf{On}$, namely every non-empty subclass has a minimum." — p. 12.

**A.2.4  $0$, $\mathrm{S}(\alpha)$, $\sup A$; limit; $\omega$; $\omega_1$; $\mathbb{N} = \omega$.** "$0 := \varnothing$ the least ordinal (**zero**); $\mathrm{S}(\alpha) := \alpha \cup \{\alpha\}$ the least ordinal $> \alpha$ (**successor** of $\alpha$); $\sup A := \bigcup_{\beta\in A} \beta$ the least ordinal $\geq \beta$ for all $\beta \in A$ (**supremum** of $A$)." — p. 12. "Let $\omega$ be the least **limit** ordinal, namely the least one that is neither a successor nor zero. We identify $\mathbb{N}$ with $\omega$. Let $\omega_1$ be the first uncountable ordinal." — p. 12. Convention: $\mathbb{N}$ includes $0$; sums run over "$n \in \mathbb{N}$" with exponents like $1/(n+1)$.

**A.2.5  Hatted classical operations $\mathbin{\hat{+}}$, $\mathbin{\hat{\cdot}}$; exponentiation $\alpha^\beta$.** "In the literature, the classical operations are usually denoted by $+$ and $\cdot$, but to prevent confusion with the field operations on the surreal numbers, we decorate them with a hat." — p. 12. Table p. 13: "**sum** $\alpha \mathbin{\hat{+}} \beta :=$ [$\alpha$ \| $\mathrm{S}(\alpha \mathbin{\hat{+}} \gamma)$ \| $\sup\{\alpha \mathbin{\hat{+}} \gamma : \gamma < \beta\}$]; **product** $\alpha \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} \beta :=$ [$0$ \| $\alpha \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} \gamma \mathbin{\hat{+}} \alpha$ \| $\sup\{\alpha \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} \gamma : \gamma < \beta\}$]; **exponentiation** $\alpha^\beta :=$ [$1$ \| $\alpha^\gamma \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} \alpha$ \| $\sup\{\alpha^\gamma : \gamma < \beta\}$]" (columns $\beta = 0$, $\beta = \mathrm{S}(\gamma)$, $\beta$ limit). Also in §1.5: "To dispel any potential ambiguity, we also use $\hat{+}$, $\hat{\cdot}$ for the classical (non-commutative) ordinal operations." — p. 7. **Convention:** exponentiation is *not* hatted; "ordinal exponentiation (only in base $\omega$)" (p. 12); "$\alpha \mapsto \omega^\alpha$ denote ordinal exponentiation in base $\omega$" (p. 7). Extension: "$-\infty \mathbin{\hat{+}} \alpha = \alpha \mathbin{\hat{+}} (-\infty) := -\infty$, $-\infty \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} \alpha = \alpha \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} (-\infty) := -\infty$, $\alpha^{-\infty} := 0$." — p. 13. **Usage count:** the hat is used in all order-type expressions ($\omega \mathbin{\hat{+}} 1$, $\omega \mathbin{\hat{+}} k$, $\omega^{\omega^\alpha} \mathbin{\hat{+}} \beta$, $\omega \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} \ell \mathbin{\hat{+}} m$) with two slips where the source prints un-hatted "$\omega + \omega + 1$" (p. 44, Example 5.5.4) and "$\omega^{\omega^\alpha} + 1$", "$\omega^2 + 1$", "$\omega^3 + 1$", "$\omega + 1$" (p. 54, §7 intro).

**A.2.6  $\varepsilon_0$.** "Note that the equation $\omega^\varepsilon = \varepsilon$ has class many solutions, the smallest of which denoted by $\varepsilon_0$." — p. 13.

**A.2.7  Cantor normal form.** "there is a unique finite sequence $\beta_1 \geq \beta_2 \geq \ldots \geq \beta_n \geq 0$ of ordinals such that $\alpha = \omega^{\beta_1} \mathbin{\hat{+}} \ldots \mathbin{\hat{+}} \omega^{\beta_n}$. (1) The expression on the right-hand side is called **Cantor normal form** of $\alpha$." — p. 13. Capitalisation varies in the source: "Cantor normal form" (pp. 12, 13, 14, 17, 27), "Cantor Normal form" (pp. 14, 45), "Cantor Normal Form" (pp. 25, 27, 29, 44, 45, 51, 56, 61). Key identities quoted as "(2) $\omega^\alpha \mathbin{\hat{+}} \omega^\beta = \omega^\beta$ if $\alpha < \beta$; (3) $(\omega^{\beta_1} \mathbin{\hat{+}} \cdots \mathbin{\hat{+}} \omega^{\beta_n}) \mathbin{\hat{\cdot}} \omega^\alpha = \omega^{(\max_i \beta_i) \hat{+} \alpha}$ if $\alpha > 0$." Phrase: "the number of terms in the Cantor Normal Form of $\deg(b)$" (pp. 44–45, 51).

**A.2.8  natural sum $\oplus$, natural product $\odot$.** "Define $\alpha \oplus \beta := \omega^{\gamma_{\pi(1)}} \mathbin{\hat{+}} \omega^{\gamma_{\pi(2)}} \mathbin{\hat{+}} \cdots \mathbin{\hat{+}} \omega^{\gamma_{\pi(n+m)}}$ (**natural sum**), $\alpha \odot \beta := \bigoplus_{1\leq i\leq n} \bigoplus_{n+1\leq j\leq n+m} \omega^{\gamma_j \oplus \gamma_j}$ (**natural product**)." — p. 13 (the exponent "$\gamma_j \oplus \gamma_j$" is as printed in the PDF; evidently intended $\gamma_i \oplus \gamma_j$ — see §G). Attributed: "$\mathbf{On}$ admits *commutative* operations introduced by Hessenberg [12]." Named "Hessenberg's natural sum" / "Hessenberg's natural product" / "Hessenberg natural operations" / "Hessenberg's arithmetic" (pp. 2, 7, 12, 14, 16, 38). "We extend the natural sum to $\mathbf{On}_\infty$ by letting $(-\infty) \oplus \alpha = \alpha \oplus (-\infty) := -\infty$." — p. 14. Fact 2.2.1: "The operations $\oplus$ and $\odot$ make $\mathbf{On}$ into an ordered semiring". Symbols $\oplus$, $\odot$ are used for these throughout (also $\oplus$ is reused p. 77 for the sum in the completion $\overline{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa$, with an explicit remark — A.17.1). Suggestive isomorphism: "$(\mathbf{On}, \oplus, \odot) \cong (\mathbb{N}[X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_\alpha, \ldots], +, \cdot)$ where we map $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$ to the variable $X_\alpha$." — p. 14.

**A.2.9  degree of an ordinal $\deg(\alpha)$.** "For $\alpha \in \mathbf{On}$, let the **degree** of $\alpha$ be the exponent $\beta_1$ in its Cantor Normal form when $\alpha > 0$, or $-\infty$ if $\alpha = 0$. In other word, $\deg(\alpha) = \max\{\beta \in \mathbf{On}_\infty : \omega^\beta \leq \alpha\}$. This justifies the notation of § 1.5: for $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ we have $\deg(b) = \deg(\operatorname{ot}(b))$." — p. 14. Fact 2.2.2: "The degree is a multiplicative valuation on $\mathbf{On}$".

**A.2.10  $B < C$; $B + C$ for subsets.** "Given an ordered set $A$ and two subsets $B, C \subseteq A$ (or more generally $A, B, C$ classes), write $B < C$ if $x < y$ for all $x \in B$ and $y \in C$. Furthermore, if $A$ is a subclass of an ordered abelian group, let $B + C := \{x + y : x \in B, y \in C\}$." — p. 14. Used constantly as "$\operatorname{supp}(c) < \operatorname{supp}(d)$", "$\operatorname{supp}(c) > x$", "$\operatorname{supp}(d) \prec \operatorname{supp}(b)$" (the $\prec$ version is extended p. 17, A.8.1).

### A.3 §2.3 Surreal numbers

**A.3.1  Working identification $\mathbf{No} = \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}))_{\mathbf{On}}$.** "For most of this paper, the reader only needs to know that $\mathbf{No} = \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{G}))_{\mathbf{On}}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive group of $\mathbf{No}$ itself. We also write $\omega$ in place of $t^{-1}$, and just write $\mathbf{No} = \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}))_{\mathbf{On}}$." — p. 15. "We shall freely identify $\mathbf{No} = \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}))_{\mathbf{On}}$." — p. 17 (after Fact 2.3.5). The approach: "We follow the approach of [9]; all the details can be found there." — p. 15.

**A.3.2  surreal number (sign-sequence definition).** "A **surreal number** is a function $s : \alpha \to 2 = \{0, 1\}$ for some ordinal $\alpha \in \mathbf{On}$. Let $\mathbf{No}$ denote the class of all surreal numbers." — p. 15. Tree picture attributed: "By [7], it is common to represent the elements of $\mathbf{No}$ as the nodes of a binary rooted tree with edges labelled by 0, 1 and paths of any ordinal length (see Fig. 1)." Fig. 1 caption: "The tree of surreal numbers." (p. 15).

**A.3.3  order $<$ on $\mathbf{No}$.** "We say that $b$ is **less than** $c$, written $b < c$, if $b(\alpha) = 0$ or $c(\alpha) = 1$. This is a dense linear order on $\mathbf{No}$." — p. 15 (with $\alpha$ "the least ordinal where they differ").

**A.3.4  simpler, $<_s$, $\leq_s$; well founded; simplicity.** "We also say that $b$ is **simpler** than $c$, written $b <_s c$, if $\operatorname{dom}(b) < \operatorname{dom}(c)$ and $b$ is the restriction of $c$ to $\operatorname{dom}(b)$. Write $b \leq_s c$ if $b <_s c$ or $b = c$." — p. 16. "This is a partial order, and it is **well founded**, namely every non-empty subclass of $\mathbf{No}$ contains $<_s$-minimal elements." — p. 16. The noun is "*Simplicity*" (p. 15: "*Simplicity* will also appear as an optional ingredient in the definition of infinite product"; p. 72: "$x$ is minimal with respect to simplicity").

**A.3.5  simplest element; $L \mid R$.** "**Fact 2.3.1.** If a non-empty subclass $C \subseteq \mathbf{No}$ is convex, namely for all $x, y \in C$ and $x < z < y$ we have $z \in C$, then the $<_s$-minimal element is unique, and we shall call it the **simplest** element of $C$. If $L, R$ are subsets of $\mathbf{No}$ with $L < R$, the convex class $\{b \in \mathbf{No} : L < b < R\}$ is non-empty, and in particular it contains a unique simplest element, denoted by $L \mid R$." — p. 16. Examples: "$0 = \varnothing \mid \varnothing, \ 1 = \{0\} \mid \varnothing, \ \frac{1}{2} = \{0\} \mid \{1\}, \ \omega = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\} \mid \varnothing$." Notation: a single vertical bar between set braces (not Conway's $\{L \mid R\}$).

**A.3.6  $L_b$, $R_b$; inductive definitions of $-b$, $b+c$.** "Given $b \in \mathbf{No}$, write $L_b := \{b' \in \mathbf{No} : b' <_s b, b' < b\}$ and $R_b := \{b'' \in \mathbf{No} : b'' <_s b, b < b''\}$. We have $b = L_b \mid R_b$." — p. 16; sum/negation formulas as displayed p. 16. Phrase: "To show that the definitions are well posed, one needs to ensure that the classes $L$ and $R$ … are sets and that $L < R$."

**A.3.7  Identification of $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbf{On}$ inside $\mathbf{No}$.** "In light of the above, we shall freely identify $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbf{On}$ with their isomorphic copies inside $\mathbf{No}$." — p. 16 (after Fact 2.3.3: reals are "The surreal numbers with either finite domain or with domain $\omega$ and not eventually constant"; ordinals "The surreal numbers that are constantly 1").

**A.3.8  $\omega^x$ (surreal omega-map); $\iota$.** "**Fact 2.3.4.** There is an inductively defined map $x \in \mathbf{No} \mapsto \omega^x \in \mathbf{No}^{>0}$ such that for all $x, y \in \mathbf{No}$, $\omega^{x+y} = \omega^x \omega^y$, and if $x < y$, then $0 \leq k\omega^x < \omega^y$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$. Moreover, the map $\alpha \mapsto \omega^\alpha$ coincides with ordinal exponentiation in base $\omega$ when $\alpha \in \mathbf{On}$." — p. 17. "One can define, by induction on the order type of a series, a natural map $\iota : \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}))_{\mathbf{On}} \to \mathbf{No}$, where the monomial $t^{-x}$ is mapped to $\omega^x$ for $x \in \mathbf{No}$. We omit its definition here." — p. 17. The paper never says "omega-map", "normal form" (for surreals), or "Conway normal form"; the canonical series expression is called "a canonical way" (p. 2) / "the identification $\mathbf{No} = \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}))_{\mathbf{On}}$".

**A.3.9  Ordinals as series: $\mathbf{On} = \mathbb{N}(-\mathbf{On})$.** "**Remark 2.3.6.** Under the identification $\mathbf{No} = \mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}))_{\mathbf{On}}$, we have $\mathbf{On} = \mathbb{N}(-\mathbf{On})$, namely the ordinal numbers are (identified with) the series with finite support, coefficients in $\mathbb{N}$, and exponents in $-\mathbf{On}$. The series corresponding to an ordinal is simply its Cantor normal form." — p. 17.

### A.4 §2.4 Archimedean classes, cofinality, fraction field

**A.4.1  $\preceq$, $\asymp$, $\prec$ (Archimedean relation); quasi-order; Archimedean class $[x]$; $\mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$.** "for $x, y \in \mathbf{G}$, we write $x \preceq y$ if there is some non-zero $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $|x| \leq n|y|$; $x \asymp y$ if $y \preceq x \preceq y$; $x \prec y$ if $x \preceq y$ and $x \not\asymp y$." — p. 17. "Then $\preceq$ is a total **quasi-order** (namely, it is a reflexive, transitive, and total), thus in particular $\asymp$ is an equivalence relation. Let $[x]$ denote the $\asymp$-equivalence class of $x \in \mathbf{G}$, and we call it the **Archimedean class** of $x$. The relation $\preceq$ induces a total order on the class of Archimedean classes $\mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$." — p. 17. **Convention:** $x \prec y$ means $x$ is *smaller* (infinitesimal relative to $y$), i.e. $\preceq$ is *magnitude* order ("the Archimedean relation"), not a valuation-theoretic order; "$x \prec y$" with $x, y \in \mathbf{G}^{<0}$ exponents means $|x| \ll |y|$. The relations are also applied to series (elements of $\mathbf{No}$, p. 76: "$\varepsilon \prec 1$", "$\omega^{x_1} \not\asymp \omega^{x_1 - \omega} \asymp c$", "$\log(\omega^{x_n}) \succ \log(\omega^\omega) \succ 1$") and to classes ("$\sigma \succ \sigma'$", "$[x] \preceq \sigma$", "non-zero $\sigma$", "$\sigma = [0]$"). Quotient notation $\mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$, $\mathbf{No}_{/\asymp}$ (subscript slash) — same style as "$K((G^{\leq 0}))_{/J}$" (p. 4), "$\mathbf{R}_{/\sim}$" (p. 34), "$\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))_{/\mathbf{J}}$" (Table 4.4.1).

**A.4.2  $\mathbf{A} \prec x$, $\mathbf{A} \prec \mathbf{B}$ etc.** "In line with our previous conventions, if $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B} \subseteq \mathbf{G}$ and $x \in \mathbf{G}$, we write $\mathbf{A} \prec x$ to mean that $y \prec x$ for all $y \in \mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{A} \prec \mathbf{B}$ if $y \prec z$ for all $y \in \mathbf{A}$ and $z \in \mathbf{B}$, and give analogous meanings to $\mathbf{A} \preceq x$, $\mathbf{A} \preceq \mathbf{B}$." — p. 17. Used as "$\operatorname{supp}(d) \prec \operatorname{supp}(b)$", "$\operatorname{supp}(c_1) \succ \sigma$", "$v(c_i) \not\prec \operatorname{supp}(b)$".

**A.4.3  $\mathbf{G}_{\prec\sigma}$, $\mathbf{G}_{\preceq\sigma}$, $\mathbf{L}_\sigma$, $\mathbf{S}_\sigma$ (Notation 2.4.1).** "Let $\sigma$ be an Archimedean class of $\mathbf{G}$. Define: the groups $\mathbf{G}_{\prec\sigma} := \{x \in \mathbf{G} : [x] \prec \sigma\}$, $\mathbf{G}_{\preceq\sigma} := \{x \in \mathbf{G} : [x] \preceq \sigma\}$ for $\sigma \neq [0]$, and $\mathbf{G}_{\prec 0} = \mathbf{G}_{\preceq 0} := \{0\}$. the field $\mathbf{L}_\sigma := \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}_{\prec\sigma}))_\kappa \subseteq \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$; the ring $\mathbf{S}_\sigma := \mathbf{L}_\sigma \cap (\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa) = \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}_{\prec\sigma}))_\kappa \subseteq \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$." — p. 18 (PDF verified: $\mathbf{Z}$ bold here; the transcription prints $\mathbb{Z}$, see §G). "Note that the above groups, fields, and rings may also be proper classes." Recap p. 64: "$H_\sigma \cong \mathbf{G}_{\preceq \sigma}/\mathbf{G}_{\prec \sigma}$ (an Archimedean group); $\mathbf{L}_\sigma = \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}_{\prec \sigma}))_\kappa \subseteq \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$ (a field); $\mathbf{S}_\sigma = \mathbf{L}_\sigma \cap (\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa) \subseteq \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ (a ring)".

**A.4.4  $H_\sigma$; $\iota_\sigma$ (Fact 2.4.2).** "(2) there is an ordered group complement $H_\sigma$ of $\mathbf{G}_{\prec\sigma}$ into $\mathbf{G}_{\preceq\sigma}$; (3) $H_\sigma$ is Archimedean when $\sigma \neq [0]$, and $H_\sigma = \{0\}$ otherwise; (4) the map $\iota_\sigma : \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}_{\preceq\sigma}))_\kappa \to \mathbf{L}_\sigma((H_\sigma))$ defined by $\iota_\sigma : b = \sum_{x\in\mathbf{G}_{\preceq\sigma}} b_x t^x \mapsto \sum_{x\in H_\sigma} t^x \left( \sum_{y\in\mathbf{G}_{\prec\sigma}} b_{x+y} t^y \right)$ is a field isomorphism; (5) the restriction of $\iota_\sigma$ to $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ is a ring isomorphism with image $\mathbf{S}_\sigma + \mathbf{L}_\sigma((H_\sigma^{<0}))$." — p. 18. Comment: "The above are just standard considerations in the theory of divisible ordered abelian groups and Hahn series. Note that since $H_\sigma$ is Archimedean, it embeds into $\mathbb{R}$, and in particular it is always a set." Phrase for the choice of embedding: "identify $H_\sigma$ with a subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$" (p. 63). **Variant:** the target ring is written both $\mathbf{L}_\sigma((H_\sigma^{\leq 0}))$ and $\mathbf{L}((H_\sigma^{\leq 0}))$ (without subscript) — the latter in Prop. 8.2.9, Def. 8.3.1, Prop. 9.2.2, Cor. 9.2.6 (PDF p. 66, 67 verified). Treat as the same object.

**A.4.5  cofinal; cofinality; cofinality $\mathbf{On}$, $\infty$.** "Given an ordered class $\mathbf{A}$ and a subclass $\mathbf{B} \subseteq \mathbf{A}$, say that $\mathbf{B}$ is **cofinal** in $\mathbf{A}$ if $\mathbf{B}$ is not bounded from above. Call **cofinality** of $\mathbf{A}$ the least cardinality $\lambda$ of a cofinal subset, if one exists. … If no subset is cofinal, but there is a cofinal subclass of the form $\{x_\alpha \mid \alpha \in \mathbf{On}\}$, we say that $\mathbf{A}$ has cofinality $\mathbf{On}$, otherwise we say that $\mathbf{A}$ has cofinality $\infty$. By convention, we write $\alpha < \mathbf{On} < \infty$." — p. 19.

**A.4.6  $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{R})$.** "given an integral domain $\mathbf{R}$, let $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{R})$ denote its fraction field." — p. 19. Result: "**Proposition 2.4.5.** $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa) = \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa = t^{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\le 0}))_\kappa$ if and only if $\mathbf{G}$ has cofinality $\ge \kappa$, or $\mathbf{G} = \{0\}$ and $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{K}$. In particular, $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{Oz}) = \mathbf{No}$ ([7, Thm. 32])." — p. 19. Notation $t^{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\le 0}))_\kappa$, $t^H \mathbf{K}((H^{\leq 0}))$ for the localisation at monomials (also p. 80).

### A.5 §2.5 Pre-Schreier and GCD domains

**A.5.1  primal (as used by the paper).** "Recall from the introduction that $b \in R$ is primal if for all $c, d \in R$, if $b$ divides $cd$, then there are $b_1, b_2 \in R$ such that $b = b_1 b_2$ and $b_1$ divides $c$, $b_2$ divides $d$. For instance, $b \in R$ is prime if and only if it is irreducible and primal." — p. 20. Introduction version (p. 5): "every series $p$ with finite support is **primal** (from [6]) in the ring $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ (Corollary 6.3.9), namely, if $p$ divides a product $cd \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, then we can write $p = p_1 p_2$ with $p_1, p_2 \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ so that $p_1$ divides $c$ and $p_2$ divides $d$." Attributed to Cohn [6] — also in B. Always "primal in [ring]" ("is primal in $\mathbf{Oz}$", "primal in $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$"), never "primal element of" except "primal elements in $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$" (p. 6, 79). Section heading: "9. On primal series".

**A.5.2  pre-Schreier (equivalence with all-primal).** "An integral domain is pre-Schreier if and only if every element is primal (this is actually the original definition from [25])." — p. 20; proof given inline. Introduction: "Integral domains with the refinement property are usually called **pre-Schreier domains** from [25]." — p. 3. Phrase: "is a pre-Schreier domain" / "is *not* pre-Schreier (so not a GCD domain)" (p. 3). Source misprint "pre-Scheier" once (p. 20).

**A.5.3  greatest common divisor; GCD domain.** "Given $b, c \in R$, we say that $d$ is a **greatest common divisor** of $b$ and $c$ if $d$ divides $b$ and $c$, and for any other $e \in R$ dividing $b$ and $c$, $e$ divides $d$. We say that $R$ is a **GCD domain** if all $b, c \in R$ have a greatest common divisor." — p. 20. Usage: always "a greatest common divisor" (indefinite article, since unique only up to units); never "gcd" as a symbol. "coprime" used without definition (p. 5 "pairwise coprime irreducible divisors"; p. 50 "$\mathrm{p}(bc)$ and $\mathrm{p}(\mathrm{rv}(b))$ are coprime").

**A.5.4  Standing facts.** "**Fact 2.5.1.** Every GCD domain is pre-Schreier (see e.g. [6, Thm. 2.5])." "**Fact 2.5.2.** For all fields $K$ and ordered abelian groups $G$, $K(G^{\le 0})$ and $K(G)$ are GCD domains, so in particular pre-Schreier domains (see e.g. [10, Thm. 6.4]). The units of $K(G^{\le 0})$ are the non-zero elements of $K$, and the units of $K(G)$ are exactly the monomials." — p. 20. Explanation idiom: "every ordered abelian group can be written as a directed union of finitely generated free abelian groups, thus $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G})$ is a directed union of unique factorisation domains by Fact 2.1.6." Spelling: "unique factorisation domain" (British -isation, throughout; "factorisation", "generalised", "localising", "emphasise").

**A.5.5  $\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{rc}}$; Open Induction.** "the ring $\mathbb{Z} + \mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{rc}}(\mathbb{Q}^{<0})$ … where $\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{rc}}$ is the field of real algebraic numbers." — p. 20, Remark 2.5.3. "Open Induction (the fragment of Peano's arithmetic with induction restricted to quantifier-free formulas)" — p. 10, Remark 2.1.2; "$\mathbf{Oz}^{\geq 0}$ is a model of *Open Induction*" (p. 2). "$\mathbf{K}^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is the algebraic closure of $\mathbf{K}$" (p. 49).

**A.5.6  Series $1+\varepsilon$ inverse; $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\ge 0}))_\kappa$ is GCD.** "there are unique $k \in \mathbf{K}$ and $\varepsilon \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{>0}))_\kappa$ such that $b = kt^{v(b)}(1 + \varepsilon)$. We then observe that $k(1 + \varepsilon)$ is invertible, with inverse $k^{-1}(1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon^2 - \dots) \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\ge 0}))_\kappa$." — p. 21, Remark 2.5.4 (attributed: "a well known fact that goes back to [17]"). Phrase: "note that we are taking *positive* exponents here".

### A.6 §2.6 Ritt's theorem (as rephrased)

**A.6.1  Ritt's theorem for $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G})$ (Prop. 2.6.2).** "Let $b \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G})$. There are $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $c_1, \dots, c_n \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G})$ such that $b = c_1 \cdots c_n$, where each $c_i$ satisfies exactly one of the following: (1) $c_i$ is irreducible and $\langle \operatorname{supp}(c_i) - v(c_i) \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has dimension $\ge 2$; (2) $\langle \operatorname{supp}(c_i) - v(c_i) \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has dimension 1; and the supports of the $c_i$'s satisfying (2) are pairwise $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent. Moreover, the factors $c_i$ as above are unique up to reordering and up to multiplication by monomials." — p. 22. Framing: "after rephrasing [22] to talk about series with finite support rather than exponential polynomials. See for instance [8] for a rephrasing of Ritt's theorem on rings of the form $R(G)$" (p. 21).

**A.6.2  Ritt's theorem for $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G}^{\le 0})$ (Prop. 2.6.3).** "Let $b \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G}^{\le 0})$. There are $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $x \in \mathbf{G}$, and $c_1, \dots, c_n \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G}^{\le 0})$ such that $b = t^x c_1 \cdots c_n$, where each $c_i$ satisfies exactly one of the following: (1) $c_i$ is irreducible and $\langle \operatorname{supp}(c_i) \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has dimension $\ge 2$; (2) $\langle \operatorname{supp}(c_i) \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has dimension 1 and $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(c_i)$; and the supports of the $c_i$'s satisfying (2) are pairwise $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent. Moreover, $x$ is unique, and the factors $c_i$ as above are unique up to reordering and up to multiplication by elements of $\mathbf{K}$." — p. 23. Claimed new: "The case of $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G}^{\le 0})$, even just for $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{G}$ sets, is slightly different and it seems to be missing from the literature." (p. 21). The phrase "unique up to reordering and up to multiplication by elements of $\mathbf{K}$" is the paper's fixed uniqueness formula (Theorem A, Thm 6.4.1, Prop 2.6.3).

**A.6.3  Dimension-1 factors; $Q(t^s)$.** "The factors in (2) and $t^r$ are the only ones that can be written as $Q(t^s)$ for some polynomial $Q$ over $\mathbf{K}$ and some $s \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$. Such factors may be irreducible, or may also have no irreducible divisors; however, their factorisation theory is easy to describe (see Remark 2.6.4)." — p. 5. Remark 2.6.4 (p. 23–24) discusses "$\mathbf{K}(\langle x \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\le 0}) \cong \bigcup_N \mathbf{K}[X^{1/N}]$" and factors "$1 - at^y$", "$1 - \zeta_N a^{1/N} t^{y/N}$", "$1 - at^y + bt^{2y}$".

### A.8 §1.5 and §3 — degree, supremum, valuations

**A.8.1  multiplicative semi-valuation; multiplicative valuation (the paper's orientation).** "$v_J : K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) \to \mathbf{On}$, which happens to be a **multiplicative semi-valuation**, namely it satisfies $v_J(bc) = v_J(b) \odot v_J(c)$ and $v_J(b + c) \leq \max\{v_J(b), v_J(c)\}$ for all $b, c \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ (see § 2.7; we use the word 'multiplicative' to emphasise that the ultrametric inequality uses the maximum, rather than the minimum which is more common in valuation theory)." — pp. 6–7, §1.5. General form p. 33, §4: "Let $w : \mathbf{R} \to M_\infty = M \cup \{-\infty\}$ be a multiplicative semi-valuation in the sense we used in § 1.5, namely a map such that for all $b, c \in \mathbf{R}$ we have (1) $w(b + c) \le \max\{w(b), w(c)\}$ (ultrametric inequality), (2) $w(bc) = w(b) + w(c)$ (multiplicativity), where by convention $m - \infty = -\infty + m = -\infty$ and $-\infty < m$ for all $m \in M_\infty$ (including $-\infty < -\infty$). Recall that $w$ is a multiplicative *valuation* when $w(b) = -\infty$ if and only if $b = 0$." **Convention:** "semi-valuation" = may send non-zero elements to the bottom value; "valuation" = bottom value only at $0$. Names of the clauses: "(ultrametric inequality)", "(multiplicativity)". Also "valued ring" ("the RV monoid of a valued ring $(\mathbf{R}, w)$", p. 33; Table title "Rings $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ arising from various valued rings"); "a true multiplicative valuation" (p. 8).

**A.8.2  degree of a series $\deg(b)$.** "**Definition.** Given $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ with $b \neq 0$, let the **degree** of $b$, denoted by $\deg(b)$, be the maximum ordinal $\alpha$ such that $\omega^\alpha \leq \operatorname{ot}(b)$. We also let $\deg(0) := -\infty$." — p. 7, §1.5 (unnumbered Definition). Explanations: "The series of degree 0 are precisely the ones with finite support, such as $t^{-\sqrt{2}} + t^{-1} + 1$, except for 0, which has degree $-\infty$. Those of degree 1 have order types between $\omega$ (included) and $\omega^2$ (excluded)." Phrases: "has degree 1", "of degree 1", "series of positive degree", "$\deg(b)$ is additively principal", "by induction on the degree" / "we reason by induction on $\deg(b)$", "the degree is a multiplicative valuation". Section 3 title: "The degree is a multiplicative valuation". **Theorem D** (p. 7): "For all non-zero $b, c \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, (1) $\deg(b + c) \leq \max\{\deg(b), \deg(c)\}$ (ultrametric inequality); (2) $\deg(bc) = \deg(b) \oplus \deg(c)$ (multiplicativity); (3) $\deg(b) = -\infty$ if and only if $b = 0$." Also "$\deg_\sigma$" (A.12.3) and "$\deg^-$" (A.11.4).

**A.8.3  supremum of a series $\sup(b)$.** "**Definition 3.1.3.** Given a non-zero $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$, let the **supremum** of $b$ be $\sup(b) := \sup(\operatorname{supp}(b))$. We also let $\sup(0) := -\infty$." — p. 26. Introduced p. 7 as "the map $\sup : \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) \to \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0} \cup \{-\infty\}$" and p. 25 as "a sibling function $\sup : \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})) \to \mathbb{R}_\infty = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$". Properties Prop. 3.5.1: "(1) $\sup(b + c) \le \max\{\sup(b), \sup(c)\}$; (2) $\sup(bc) = \sup(b) + \sup(c)$; (3) $\sup(b) = -\infty$ if and only if $b = 0$." Phrases: "$\sup(b) = 0$", "0 an accumulation point of $\operatorname{supp}(b)$" (p. 8, for Berarducci's hypothesis), "support with supremum 0". Variants: $\sup'$ (Remark 3.5.2, supremum in the Dedekind–MacNeille completion), $\sup_\sigma$ (A.12.3), and the class-valued $\sup$ of §8.5 (A.17.3).

**A.8.4  Relationship $v_J \le \omega^{\deg} \le \operatorname{ot}$.** "We have that $v_J(b) \leq \omega^{\deg(b)} \leq \operatorname{ot}(b)$ for all series $b$, but both inequalities are often strict" — p. 7; Remark 2.7.2 p. 25.

**A.8.5  Countability of order types for $\mathbf{G}=\mathbb{R}$ (Remark 3.1.5).** "For $\mathbf{G} = \mathbb{R}$, the maps ot and deg take values among the *countable* ordinals." … "The set of countable ordinals is denoted by $\omega_1$, which is itself an ordinal (the least uncountable one)." — p. 26. Consequence used as convention: the value monoid of $\deg$ is "$M = \omega_1$ equipped with Hessenberg's natural sum" (p. 38), and $\alpha \in \omega_1$ is the standing range of degrees in §5–§7.

### A.9 §3.2–§3.3 Truncations and normal forms

**A.9.1  truncations (two senses).** Informal: "When a series $b$ is written as a sum $b = c + d$ as in the above proposition, we shall call $c$ and $d$ *truncations* of $b$." — p. 26 (after Prop. 3.2.1: "$\operatorname{supp}(c) < \operatorname{supp}(d)$, $\operatorname{ot}(c) = \alpha$, $\operatorname{ot}(d) = \beta$ and $b = c + d$"). Formal: "**Definition 3.2.2.** Given $b = \sum_x b_x t^x \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$ and $y \in \mathbf{G}$, we let the **truncations** of $b$ at $y$ be: $b_{\le y} := \sum_{x\le y} b_x t^x, \quad b_{<y} := \sum_{x<y} b_x t^x, \quad b_{\ge y} := \sum_{x\ge y} b_x t^x, \quad b_{>y} := \sum_{x>y} b_x t^x$." — p. 27. Note the paper's "truncation" includes *upper* truncations ($b_{\ge y}$, $b_{>y}$) as well as lower. Identities: "$b = b_{<y} + b_{\ge y} = b_{\le y} + b_{>y}$, as well as $\operatorname{ot}(b) = \operatorname{ot}(b_{<y}) \mathbin{\hat{+}} \operatorname{ot}(b_{\ge y})$". Verb: "After truncating both sides at $x + y$" (p. 30). Also $(bc)_{\le x}$ for truncation of a product. In §8, "$T_\sigma(b)$ and $\tau_\sigma(b)$ are truncations of $b$ in the sense of Definition 3.2.2" (Remark 8.1.2, p. 62).

**A.9.2  additively principal (ordinal); weakly principal; principal (series).** "**Definition 3.3.1.** An ordinal $\alpha \in \mathbf{On}$ is **additively principal** if $\alpha = \omega^\beta$ for some $\beta \in \mathbf{On}$; equivalently, by equation (2) on page 13, if $\alpha = \beta \mathbin{\hat{+}} \gamma$ implies $\beta = 0$ or $\gamma = 0$ for all $\beta, \gamma \in \mathbf{On}$, and $\alpha \neq 0$. A series $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$ is **weakly principal** if $\operatorname{ot}(b)$ is additively principal. A series $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ is **principal** if it is weakly principal and $\sup(b) = 0$." — p. 27. Also "multiplicatively principal, i.e. of the form $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$" (p. 54, said of order types). "principal" is the paper's central adjective (well over 100 uses): "principal series", "$b$ principal", "$B$ is principal" (for RV elements, A.10.8), "principal elements", "the submonoid of principal elements" (§5.2 heading), "the subring of principal elements" (§6.1 heading). Remark 3.3.8: "principal series do not form a ring, because the sum of two principal series may not be principal."

**A.9.3  weak normal form.** "**Definition 3.3.2.** Given $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$, we call the sum $b = b_1 + \dots + b_n$ the **weak normal form** of $b$ when: $b_i$ is weakly principal for all $i = 1, \dots, n$; $\operatorname{ot}(b_1) \ge \dots \ge \operatorname{ot}(b_n)$; $\operatorname{supp}(b_1) < \dots < \operatorname{supp}(b_n)$." — p. 27. Prop. 3.3.4: "For all series $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$ there exists a unique weak normal form."

**A.9.4  normal form (of a series in $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}))$).** "**Definition 3.3.6.** Given $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}))$, we call the sum $b = b_1 t^{x_1} + \dots + b_n t^{x_n}$ the **normal form** of $b$ when: $x_1 \le \dots \le x_n$; $b_i$ is principal for all $i = 1, \dots, n$; $\operatorname{ot}(b_1) \ge \dots \ge \operatorname{ot}(b_n)$; $\operatorname{supp}(t^{x_i} b_i) < \operatorname{supp}(t^{x_{i+1}} b_{i+1})$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n-1$." — p. 28. Prop. 3.3.7: "For all series $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ there exists a unique normal form." Phrase: "write $b = b_1 t^{x_1} + \dots + b_n t^{x_n}$ in normal form" (pp. 29, 32, 40, 42). Remark 3.3.8 gloss: "any series in $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ can be intuitively construed as a series with coefficients in the set of principal series, exponents in $\mathbb{R}$, and *finite support*." Note: the *series* normal form puts the principal coefficient on the left, $b_i t^{x_i}$ (not $t^{x_i}b_i$), with $x_i$ increasing; equality of consecutive $x_i$ is possible (Remark 3.3.10: "the exponent 0 appears twice in the normal form" for $1 + \sum t^{-1/(n+1)}$).

**A.9.5  Fact 3.4.1 (Berarducci) and its use.** "**Fact 3.4.1** ([2, Corollary 9.9]). If $b, c \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ are weakly principal, then $\operatorname{ot}(bc) = \operatorname{ot}(b) \odot \operatorname{ot}(c)$." — p. 29. Gloss: "the order type function ot is multiplicative on weakly principal series"; "for a principal series $b$ we have $\operatorname{ot}(b) = v_J(b)$" (p. 29; proved as Prop. 7.2.1). Referred to as "Berarducci's Fact 3.4.1" (p. 31).

**A.9.6  Failure of $\deg$ and $\sup$ for general $\mathbf{G}$ (Remarks 3.4.4, 3.5.2).** "The map $b \mapsto \deg(\operatorname{ot}(b))$ is well defined on $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\le 0}))_\kappa$ for any group $\mathbf{G}$, but it fails to be multiplicative as soon as $\mathbf{G}$ does not embed into $\mathbb{R}$." — p. 31. Counterexample: "$b = t^y \cdot \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}} t^{-nx}, \quad c = 1 - t^x$" with "$x \prec y$".

### A.10 §4 The quotient monoid RV

**A.10.1  Setting.** "In this section, let $\mathbf{R}$ be a commutative ring, possibly a proper class, and $(M, +)$ be an ordered commutative monoid." — p. 33. "We shall construct a monoid RV by taking an appropriate quotient of the multiplicative monoid of $\mathbf{R}$, and an auxiliary ring $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ containing RV as multiplicative submonoid. This will eventually be applied to $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ and $w = \deg$." — p. 33. Framing: "We first build the RV monoid of a valued ring $(\mathbf{R}, w)$, generalising the construction of the RV group of valued fields." — p. 33 (no citation for "RV group"; see C).

**A.10.2  $\sim$.** "**Definition 4.1.1.** Given $b, c \in \mathbf{R}$, we write $b \sim c$ if $w(b - c) < w(b)$." — p. 33. Prop. 4.1.5: "The relation $\sim$ is a congruence, that is, for all $b, c, d \in \mathbf{R}$, if $c \sim d$, then $bc \sim bd$."

**A.10.3  RV, $\operatorname{rv}$.** "**Definition 4.1.4.** Let RV be the quotient $\mathbf{R}_{/\sim}$, and let $\operatorname{rv} : \mathbf{R} \to \mathrm{RV}$ be the natural quotient map. Given $b \in \mathbf{R}$, we define $w(\operatorname{rv}(b)) := w(b)$." — p. 34. Typography: upright "RV" (monoid), upright lower-case "rv" (map). Elements of RV are capital italic $B, C, D$ ("Given $B, C \in \mathrm{RV}$"); elements of $\mathbf{R}$ lower-case. Product: "**Definition 4.1.6.** Given $B, C \in \mathrm{RV}$, we let $B \cdot C := \operatorname{rv}(bc)$, where $\operatorname{rv}(b) = B$, $\operatorname{rv}(c) = C$." — p. 34; the dot $\cdot$ is written explicitly for RV products ("$B \cdot C$", "$\operatorname{rv}(b_1) \cdot t^{x_1}$", "$C_1 \cdot q_1$"). Prop. 4.1.7: "a commutative monoid with zero (with multiplicative identity $\operatorname{rv}(1)$ and absorbing element $\operatorname{rv}(0)$)". Later, in §5: "$\operatorname{rv} : \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})) \to \mathrm{RV}$ will denote the multiplicative, degree-preserving quotient map" (p. 38).

**A.10.4  $\mathrm{RV}_m$.** "**Definition 4.2.1.** Given $m \in M$, let $\mathrm{RV}_m := \{B \in \mathrm{RV} : w(B) = m\} \cup \{\operatorname{rv}(0)\}$." — p. 34. Partial sum: "**Definition 4.2.4.** Given $m \in M$ and $B, C \in \mathrm{RV}_m$, we define $B + C := \begin{cases} \mathrm{rv}(b+c) & \text{if } w(b+c) = w(b), \\ \mathrm{rv}(0) & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}$ where $b, c \in \mathbf{R}$ are such that $B = \mathrm{rv}(b)$ and $C = \mathrm{rv}(c)$." — p. 35. Results: "the sum $+$ makes $\mathrm{RV}_m$ into an abelian group" (Prop. 4.2.6); "$\mathrm{RV}_m$ is an $\mathrm{RV}_0$-module" (Cor. 4.2.9); heading "4.2. Modules in RV". In §5 the modules are $\mathrm{RV}_\alpha$ with $\alpha \in \omega_1$.

**A.10.5  $\mathcal{O}$, $\mathcal{M}$, $\mathrm{res}$; residue ring.** "Let $\mathcal{O} = \{b \in \mathbf{R} : w(b) \le 0\}$, $\mathcal{M} = \{b \in \mathbf{R} : w(b) < 0\}$. Let $\mathrm{res} : \mathcal{O} \to \mathrm{RV}_0$ be defined as $\mathrm{res}(b) = \mathrm{rv}(b)$ if $b \notin \mathcal{M}$ and $\mathrm{res}(b) = \mathrm{rv}(0)$ otherwise." — p. 36, Prop. 4.2.10. "**Definition 4.2.11.** We call $\mathrm{RV}_0$ the **residue ring** of $(\mathbf{R}, w)$." — p. 36. Also "$\mathcal{O}_m$", "$\mathcal{M}_m$" (p. 36). Note the orientation: with multiplicative valuations the valuation ring is $w \le 0$.

**A.10.6  $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$.** "**Definition 4.3.1.** Let $(\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}, +)$ be the direct sum $\bigoplus_{m \in M}(\mathrm{RV}_m, +)$. We write its elements as $\sum_{m \in M} B_m$, where $B_m \in \mathrm{RV}_m$ for all $m \in M$, and $B_m \neq \mathrm{rv}(0)$ for at most finitely many values of $m$." — p. 37; product "$\left(\sum_{m \in M} B_m\right) \cdot \left(\sum_{m \in M} C_m\right) := \sum_{m \in M} \left(\sum_{n+o=m} B_n \cdot C_o\right)$." "**Notation 4.3.2.** $\mathrm{RV}$ embeds naturally into $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ … so we shall identify $\mathrm{RV}$ with its isomorphic copy in $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$." — p. 37. Universal property claimed: "any map from $\mathrm{RV}$ to a ring preserving sum and product factors through $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$" (p. 37). "**Definition 4.3.4.** We extend $w : \mathrm{RV} \to M_\infty$ to $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ as follows: for every non-zero $B = \sum_m B_m \in \widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$, let $w(B) := \max\{m \in M : B_m \neq \mathrm{rv}(0)\}$, and let $w(0) := -\infty$." — p. 37. Heading "4.3. Embedding RV into a ring".

**A.10.7  $\mathrm{RV}^*$; Table 4.4.1.** "if we let $\mathrm{RV}^* := \mathrm{RV} \setminus \{\mathrm{rv}(0)\}$, the monoid $\mathrm{RV}^*$ is a group sitting in the exact sequence $1 \to \mathrm{RV}_0^\times \to \mathrm{RV}^* \to M \to 0$" — p. 38 (when $\mathbf{R}$ is a field). Table 4.4.1 (p. 38) rows: $(\mathbb{Q}, v_p)$, $(\mathbb{Z}, v_p)$, $(\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})), v)$, $(\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})), \sup)$, $(\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})), \deg)$, $(\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})), v_J)$; columns $\mathrm{RV}_0$, $\mathrm{RV}^*$, $\mathrm{RV}_m$, $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$. For $\deg$: $\mathrm{RV}_0 = \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$, $\mathrm{RV}_m = \mathrm{P}_m \otimes_{\mathbf{K}} \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$, $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}} = \widehat{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$. For $v_J$: $\mathrm{RV}_0 = \mathbf{K}$, $\mathrm{RV}^* = \mathrm{P}$, $\mathrm{RV}_m = \mathrm{P}_\alpha$ (for $m = \omega^\alpha$), $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}} = \widehat{\mathrm{P}}$.

**A.10.8  Standing identification in §5–§7.** "**Notation 5.1.2.** With a slight abuse of notation, we shall identify $\mathrm{RV}_0$ with $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$, and for instance write $0$ in place of $\mathrm{rv}(0)$. In particular, we shall also say that each $\mathrm{RV}_\alpha$ is a $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$-module." — p. 39 (after Prop. 5.1.1: "The residue ring $\mathrm{RV}_0$ is the image $\mathrm{rv}(\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$, and the restriction $\mathrm{rv}_{\restriction \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})} : \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}) \to \mathrm{RV}_0$ is a ring isomorphism."). Consequence: series with finite support $p, q$ are freely treated as elements of RV / $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ ("$p$ divides $q$ in $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$", "$B \cdot p$", "$B = C_1 \cdot q_1 + \cdots + C_k \cdot q_k$").

### A.11 §5–§6 Principal elements, maximal divisor, $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}$

**A.11.1  principal (RV element); $\mathrm{P}$; $\mathrm{P}_\alpha$.** "**Definition 5.2.1.** Given $B \in \mathrm{RV}$, we say that $B$ is **principal** if $B = \mathrm{rv}(b)$ for some principal $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$. Let $\mathrm{P}$ denote the subset of $\mathrm{RV}$ consisting of $0$ and of all principal elements of $\mathrm{RV}$. Given $\alpha \in \omega_1$, we define $\mathrm{P}_\alpha := \mathrm{P} \cap \mathrm{RV}_\alpha$." — p. 39. Example 5.2.2: "$\mathrm{P}_0 = \mathbf{K}$". Props 5.2.3–5.2.5: "$\mathrm{P}$ is closed under the multiplication of $\mathrm{RV}$"; "$\mathrm{P}_\alpha$ is a $\mathbf{K}$-linear subspace of $\mathrm{RV}_\alpha$". Remark 7.2.4: "the $\mathbf{K}$-vector space $\mathrm{P}_\alpha$ can be alternatively presented as the quotient $\mathbf{J}_{\omega^{\alpha+1}}/\mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha}$ … In particular, $\mathrm{P}$ is the RV monoid of the semi-valuation $v_J$." — p. 58.

**A.11.2  extension of scalars; $\tilde\mu$.** "The multiplication map $\mu : \mathrm{P}_\alpha \times \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}) \to \mathrm{RV}_\alpha$ given by $(B, p) \mapsto B \cdot p$ is $\mathbf{K}$-linear in both arguments. Thus, it has a canonical extension to a $\mathbf{K}$-linear map $\tilde{\mu} : \mathrm{P}_\alpha \otimes_{\mathbf{K}} \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}) \to \mathrm{RV}_\alpha$ … We can show that $\tilde{\mu}$ is an isomorphism, and thus that $\mathrm{RV}_\alpha$ is the **extension of scalars** of $\mathrm{P}_\alpha$ from $\mathbf{K}$ to $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$." — p. 40. Prop. 5.3.1: "the map $\tilde{\mu} : \mathrm{P}_\alpha \otimes_{\mathbf{K}} \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}) \to \mathrm{RV}_\alpha$ is an isomorphism." Unique representation: "$B_1 \otimes t^{x_1} + \cdots + B_n \otimes t^{x_n}$ where $x_1 < \cdots < x_n \le 0$ are distinct real numbers and $B_1, \ldots, B_n$ are non-zero elements of $\mathrm{P}_\alpha$" (p. 40); in RV written "$B = B_1 \cdot t^{x_1} + \cdots + B_n \cdot t^{x_n}$" and called "in normal form" (Remark 5.4.7, p. 42). Script letter $\mathcal{B}$ for tensor elements.

**A.11.3  divides in RV, $B \mid C$.** "**Definition 5.4.1.** Given $B, C \in \mathrm{RV}$, we say that $B$ **divides** $C$, and write $B \mid C$, if $C = B \cdot D$ for some $D \in \mathrm{RV}$." — p. 41. In $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$: "Given $B, C \in \widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$, we say that $B$ **divides** $C$, and write $B \mid C$, if $C = B \cdot D$ for some $D \in \widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$." — p. 46, §6.2; "divisibility in $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ is just an extension of the notion of divisibility in Definition 5.4.1, so there is no risk of ambiguity." Cor. 6.2.3 reconciles with $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le0})$-divisibility. In the base ring, "$b$ divides $c$", "$q \mid b$", "$b/c$", "$c/b \in R$ to mean that there is some $e \in R$ such that $de = c$" (p. 81, Lemma 9.2.1: "In a small abuse of notation").

**A.11.4  maximal divisor with finite support; $\mathrm{p}(B)$, $\mathrm{p}(b)$.** Heading "5.4. The maximal divisor with finite support for RV" / "5.5. … for $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$". Prop. 5.4.3: "For all $B \in \mathrm{RV}$, there exists a series $p \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$ such that for all $q \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$, $q \mid B$ if and only if $q \mid p$. Moreover, $p$ is unique up to multiplication by non-zero elements of $\mathbf{K}$, and $p \in \mathbf{K}$ if $B$ is principal." — p. 41. "**Notation 5.4.5.** Given a non-zero $B \in \widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$, if $p$ is an element of $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$ satisfying the conclusion of Corollary 5.4.4, then $kp$ also satisfies the conclusion for any non-zero $k \in \mathbf{K}$. Therefore, there is exactly one choice of $p$ such that the coefficient of $t^{\sup(p)}$ is $1$. Let $\mathrm{p}(B)$ be this element. We also let $\mathrm{p}(0) := 0$." — p. 42. "**Notation 5.5.2.** Just as in Notation 5.4.5, given a non-zero $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$, let $\mathrm{p}(b)$ be the unique divisor satisfying the conclusion of Proposition 5.5.1, namely $q \mid b$ if and only if $q \mid \mathrm{p}(b)$ for all $q \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$, such that the coefficient of $t^{\sup(p)}$ is $1$. We also let $\mathrm{p}(0) := 0$." — p. 43. Normalisation convention: leading (top-exponent) coefficient 1. Names used: "the maximal divisor with finite support $\mathrm{p}(b_0)$" (p. 44), "a maximal divisor $p$ with finite support, which is unique up to multiplication by elements of $\mathbf{K}$" (p. 5), "the maximal divisor of degree 0" (p. 55), "the maximal divisor in $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$" (p. 44). Key facts: "$\mathrm{p}(B) = 1$ whenever $B \in \mathrm{RV}$ is principal" (Remark 5.4.6); "$\mathrm{p}(bc) = \mathrm{p}(b)\,\mathrm{p}(c)$" (Prop. 6.3.8); "for all $B \in \mathrm{RV}$ and non-zero $C \in \mathrm{P}$ we have $\mathrm{p}(B \cdot C) = \mathrm{p}(B)$" (Lemma 6.3.1). Also $\deg^-$: "let $\deg^-(B)$ be the smallest $\alpha$ such that $B_\alpha \neq 0$. We also set $\deg^-(0) = +\infty$." — p. 46 (proof of Prop. 6.2.1; note $+\infty$ here).

**A.11.5  Algorithmic remarks.** "The proof of Proposition 5.4.3 is also an algorithm to compute the maximal divisor of $\mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$." — p. 42, Remark 5.4.7; "The above argument has an algorithmic flavour … However, we do not know an algorithm to determine when a series with infinite support is irreducible, and the task seems highly not trivial." — p. 45.

**A.11.6  Prop. 5.6.1 / Thm 6.4.1 (existence + bound + uniqueness).** "**Theorem 6.4.1.** For all non-zero $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$, there exist $n \in \mathbb{N}$, irreducible series $c_1, \ldots, c_n \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ with infinite support, and $p \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$ such that $b = pc_1 \cdots c_n$. Moreover, $n$ is at most the number of terms in the Cantor Normal Form of $\deg(b)$, and $p$ is unique up to multiplication by elements of $\mathbf{K}$." — p. 51. Remark 5.6.2: "The bound on $n$ is sharp." Section titles: "5.6. The factorisation", "6.4. Uniqueness of the factor with finite support".

**A.11.7  $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}$; principal in $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$.** "**Definition 6.1.1.** An element $\sum_\alpha B_\alpha$ of $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ is **principal** if each $B_\alpha$ is either zero or principal (namely in $\mathrm{P}$). Let $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}$ denote the set of all principal elements of $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$." — p. 46. "Note that $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}$ is precisely the subring of $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ generated by $\mathrm{P}$, and it contains $\mathrm{rv}(1)$." Prop. 6.1.2 and identification: "we can canonically identify $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ with $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$, the ring of the series with coefficients in $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}$, exponents in $\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}$, and finite support." — p. 46. Remark 6.1.3: "$\widehat{\mathrm{P}}^{-1} \cdot \widehat{\mathrm{RV}} = \mathrm{Frac}(\widehat{\mathrm{P}}) \cdot \widehat{\mathrm{RV}} = \mathrm{Frac}(\widehat{\mathrm{P}})(\mathbb{R}^{\le 0})$", "obtained by localising $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$ with respect to the multiplicative subset $\widehat{\mathrm{P}} \setminus \{0\}$". $\mathbf{L} = \mathrm{Frac}(\widehat{\mathrm{P}})$ as local notation (p. 49). "$\widehat{\mathrm{P}}_B$ denote the ring $\widehat{\mathrm{P}}$ localised with respect to the complement of the ideal generated by $B$" (p. 61).

**A.11.8  relatively algebraically closed.** "$\mathbf{K}$ is relatively algebraically closed in $\mathrm{Frac}(\widehat{\mathrm{P}})$, that is, every element of $\mathrm{Frac}(\widehat{\mathrm{P}})$ which is algebraic over $\mathbf{K}$ is already in $\mathbf{K}$." — p. 48, Lemma 6.3.3.

**A.11.9  Corollary 6.4.2 (equivalences).** "The following are equivalent: (1) $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ is a pre-Schreier domain; (2) $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$ is a GCD domain; (3) in $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le 0}))$, every irreducible series with infinite support is prime." — p. 51. §7 opening: "it is still an open question whether all irreducible series of $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ are prime" (p. 54).

**A.11.10  almost irreducible (for $\mathbf{K}((H^{\le 0}))$).** "we shall say that a series $b \in \mathbf{K}((H^{\leq 0}))$ is **almost irreducible** if for every divisor $c \in \mathbf{K}((H^{\leq 0}))$ of $b$, if $c$ is not a monomial, then $b/c$ is." — p. 52, §6.5 ("6.5. The non-complete case"). Setting: "$H$ is a divisible subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$, which may or may not be all of $\mathbb{R}$; equivalently, $H$ is a divisible Archimedean group, possibly non-complete." Remark 6.5.1: "Clearly, all irreducible series are almost irreducible. Moreover, if $b$ is almost irreducible and $\sup(b) = 0$, then $b$ is irreducible". Lifted to $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ in Def. 8.3.1 (A.13.1).

**A.11.11  $p_H$; $\mathrm{p}(b)_H$.** "**Lemma 6.5.2.** For all $p \in \mathbf{K}(\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})$, there exists a unique $p_H \in 1 + \mathbf{K}(H^{<0})$ such that for all $q \in 1 + \mathbf{K}(H^{<0})$, $q$ divides $p$ if and only if $q$ divides $p_H$." — p. 52. Then "$\mathrm{p}(b)_H$" (Cor. 6.5.3, 6.5.5). Normalisation here: constant term 1 (element of $1 + \mathbf{K}(H^{<0})$), not top coefficient 1.

**A.11.12  Theorem 6.5.7.** "Let $b \in \mathbf{K}((H^{\leq 0}))$ with $b \neq 0$. Then there are $x \in H^{\leq 0}$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $c_1, \ldots, c_n \in \mathbf{K}((H^{\leq 0}))$, and a unique $p \in 1 + \mathbf{K}(H^{<0})$ such that $b = pt^x c_1 \cdots c_n$, where each $c_i$ is almost irreducible with infinite support. If moreover $\sup(b) \in H$, then we may take $c_1, \ldots, c_n$ irreducible, in which case $x$ is unique (and equal to $\sup(b)$)." — p. 53.

### A.12 §7 Irreducibility and primality criteria

**A.12.1  Criteria (own results), statement forms.** "**Corollary 7.1.2.** For all $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, if $\deg(b)$ is additively principal and $\mathrm{p}(b) = 1$, then $b$ is irreducible." — p. 55. "**Theorem E.** For all $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, if the order type of $b$ is of the form $\omega^{\omega^\alpha} \mathbin{\hat{+}} \beta$ with $\beta < \omega^{\omega^\alpha}$, and $b$ is not divisible by $t^x$ for any $x \in \mathbb{R}^{<0}$, then $b$ is irreducible." — p. 8. "**Theorem F.** For all $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, if the order type of $b$ is of the form $\omega \mathbin{\hat{+}} k$ with $k < \omega$, and $b$ is not divisible by $t^x$ for any $x \in \mathbb{R}^{<0}$, then $b$ is prime." — p. 8. "**Lemma 7.2.9.** For all $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, if $\mathrm{rv}(b)/\mathrm{p}(\mathrm{rv}(b))$ is prime (in $\widehat{\mathrm{RV}}$) and $\mathrm{p}(b) = 1$, then $b$ is prime." — p. 59. Hypothesis idiom: "$b$ is not divisible by $t^x$ for any $x \in \mathbb{R}^{<0}$" (equivalently "$\sup(b) = 0$"); the paper phrases Berarducci's/Pitteloud's condition as "with 0 an accumulation point of $\operatorname{supp}(b)$" (p. 8) or "$\sup\{r_i : i < \omega^{\omega^\beta}\} = 0$" (p. 3).

**A.12.2  $\mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha}$; $b \mid c \mod \mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha}$.** "**Definition 7.2.2** ([18, p. 1209]). Given $\alpha \in \omega_1$, let $\mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha}$ be the $\mathbf{K}$-vector space $\mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha} := \{e \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) : v_J(e) < \omega^\alpha\}$. Moreover, write $b \mid c \mod \mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha}$ if there exists $d \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ such that $c \equiv bd \mod \mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha}$." — p. 57 (attributed to Pitteloud; also in B.2). "For clarity, note for instance that $\mathbf{J}_{\omega^0} = \mathbf{J}_1 = \mathbf{J}$, $\mathbf{J}_{\omega^1} = \mathbf{J}_\omega = \mathbf{J} + \mathbf{K}$." Phrase "divisibility modulo $\mathbf{J}_{\omega^\alpha}$" (p. 58).

**A.12.3  Examples of new irreducibles / primes.** Example 7.1.5: "$b = \left(\sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}} t^{x/(3n+1)}\right) t^{-3} + \left(\sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}} t^{x/(3n+2)}\right) t^{-2} + t^{(k-1)x/k} + \cdots + t^{x/k} + 1$ is an irreducible series of order type $\omega \mathbin{\hat{+}} \omega \mathbin{\hat{+}} k \mathbin{\hat{+}} 1$ for every $k \in \mathbb{N}$." — p. 56 (the source says "For any $x \in \mathbf{G}^{<0}$" though the ring is $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le0}))$; Example 7.2.14 re-writes the same series with $t^{3x}$, $t^{2x}$ in place of $t^{-3}$, $t^{-2}$ — inconsistency in source, see §G). Prop. 7.2.13: "For every $\alpha \in \omega_1$, if $\deg(\alpha) = 1$, then there is a prime series $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ such that $\operatorname{ot}(b) = \alpha$." Conjecture-like remark p. 57: "It is reasonable to expect that for *every* $\alpha \in \omega_1$ there is an irreducible series $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ with order type $\operatorname{ot}(b) = \alpha$."

### A.13 §8 From real exponents to omnific integers

**A.13.1  $T_\sigma$, $\tau_\sigma$.** "**Definition 8.1.1.** Let $\sigma \in \mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$. Given $b = \sum_{x \in \mathbf{G}} b_x t^x \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$, let $T_\sigma$, $\tau_\sigma$ be the following functions from $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ to itself: $T_\sigma(b) := \sum_{[x] \preceq \sigma} b_x t^x, \quad \tau_\sigma(b) := \sum_{[x] \prec \sigma} b_x t^x$." — p. 62. Prop. 8.1.3: "$T_\sigma$ and $\tau_\sigma$ are ring homomorphisms." Section heading "8.1. Translating to real powers". No names given to $T_\sigma$, $\tau_\sigma$ beyond "the maps $T_\sigma$ and $\tau_\sigma$".

**A.13.2  $\deg_\sigma$, $\sup_\sigma$.** "**Corollary 8.1.4.** For all non-zero $\sigma \in \mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$, identify $H_\sigma$ with a subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$, and let $\deg_\sigma$ and $\sup_\sigma$ be the maps defined as follows for $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$, with $\iota_\sigma$ from Fact 2.4.2: $\deg_\sigma(b) := \deg(\iota_\sigma(T_\sigma(b))) \in \omega_1, \quad \sup_\sigma(b) := \sup(\iota_\sigma(T_\sigma(b))) \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $\deg_\sigma$ and $\sup_\sigma$ are multiplicative semi-valuations." — p. 63. "Note that the value of $\deg_\sigma$ does not depend on the embedding of $H_\sigma$ into $\mathbb{R}$."

**A.13.3  $\rho_\sigma$.** "**Definition 8.2.4.** Let $\sigma \in \mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$. Define $\rho_\sigma : \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa \to \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ as $\rho_\sigma(b) := \begin{cases} T_\sigma(b)/\tau_\sigma(b) & \text{if } \tau_\sigma(b) \neq 0, \\ T_\sigma(b) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$" — p. 65. "Moreover, note that we always have $\rho_\sigma \circ \rho_\sigma = \rho_\sigma$." Section heading "8.2. Reducing divisors". Gloss: "It is convenient to remove this source of divisibility, as follows." (p. 65). "In particular, $\rho_\sigma$ takes values in $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\sigma^{<0}))_\kappa$, or more precisely in $\mathbf{K}((\sigma^{<0}))_\kappa \cup (1 + \mathbf{K}((\sigma^{<0}))_\kappa)$." (p. 65; here $\sigma$ itself is used as an exponent set).

**A.13.4  reduced.** "**Definition 8.2.6.** A non-zero $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ is **reduced** if $\operatorname{supp}(b) \cap \operatorname{supp}(b - 1)$ is fully contained in one Archimedean class." — p. 66. Introduction version: "We call an omnific integer $b \in \mathbf{Oz}$ *reduced* if $\operatorname{supp}(b) \cap \operatorname{supp}(b-1)$ is contained in a single Archimedean class (see Definition 8.2.6)." — p. 5. Equivalents in Prop. 8.2.5 (1)–(5), e.g. "(4) $\tau_\sigma(b) \in \{0, 1\}$ for $\sigma = [v(b)]$". Phrases: "reduced omnific integers", "reduced series", "reduced factors", "the associated reduced series" (p. 70), "reduced integers" (p. 5, once). Examples p. 5: "$\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}} \omega^{1/(n+1)}$, $\omega^{\sqrt{2}} + \omega + 1$, $3$ are reduced, while $\omega^\omega + \omega^{\sqrt{2}}$, $\omega^{\sqrt{2}} + \omega + 3$ are not." Motivation: "A key reason for this choice is that every omnific integer admits reduced factors".

**A.13.5  pseudo-support $\overline{\operatorname{supp}}(b)$; pseudo-polynomial; pseudo-monomial; pseudo-irreducible; almost irreducible.** "**Definition 8.3.1.** Given a reduced $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa \setminus \mathbf{Z}$ with $\sigma = [v(b)]$: the **pseudo-support** of $b$, denoted by $\overline{\operatorname{supp}}(b)$, is the support of $\iota_\sigma(b)$ (a subset of $H_\sigma \subseteq \mathbb{R}$); $b$ is a **pseudo-polynomial** if its pseudo-support is finite; $b$ is a **pseudo-monomial** if its pseudo-support consists of exactly one point; $b$ is **pseudo-irreducible** if $\iota_\sigma(b)$ is irreducible in $\mathbf{L}((H_\sigma^{\leq 0}))$; $b$ is **almost irreducible** if $\iota_\sigma(b)$ is almost irreducible in $\mathbf{L}((H_\sigma^{\leq 0}))$ (in the sense of § 6.5)." — p. 67 (PDF verified; overline over "supp"). Introduction gloss: "*pseudo-polynomials*, which are reduced omnific integers identified with series with finite support in $\mathbb{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{<0}))$ (Definition 8.3.1). Reduced omnific integers with finite support, such as $\omega^{\sqrt{2}} + \omega + 1$, are examples of pseudo-polynomials." — pp. 5–6. Hyphenated throughout ("pseudo-polynomial", never "pseudopolynomial"). Heading "8.3. Pseudo-irreducibles and pseudo-polynomials". Example 8.3.2 computes pseudo-supports in $\mathbf{Oz}$: "$\omega^2 + \omega + 1$, $\sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \omega^{\omega + 1/(n+1)} + \omega^\omega + 1$ are pseudo-polynomials of pseudo-supports respectively $\{-2, -1, 0\}$, $\{-1, 0\}$; similarly, $\sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \omega^{3\omega + 1/(n+1)}$ is a pseudo-monomial since its pseudo-support is $\{-3\}$." Convention there: "we choose the isomorphism so that $\omega^{y_{0,0}}$ is mapped to 1."

**A.13.6  Ritt's theorem for pseudo-polynomials (Prop. 8.3.3).** "Let $p$ be a pseudo-polynomial in $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$. There are an integer $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and pseudo-polynomials $m, c_1, \ldots, c_n \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ such that $p = mc_1 \cdots c_n$, where $m$ is a pseudo-monomial, each $c_i$ satisfies exactly one of the following: (1) $c_i$ is irreducible and $\langle \overline{\operatorname{supp}}(c_i) \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has dimension $\geq 2$; (2) $\langle \overline{\operatorname{supp}}(c_i) \rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has dimension 1 and $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(c_i)$; and the pseudo-supports of the $c_i$'s satisfying (2) are pairwise $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent. Moreover, the factors $c_i$ and $m$ as above are unique up to reordering." — pp. 67–68.

**A.13.7  Prop. 8.3.7 (factorisation of reduced series).** "Let $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa \setminus \mathbf{Z}$ be reduced. Then there are $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and reduced $p, m, c_1, \ldots, c_n \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ such that $b = pmc_1 \cdots c_n$, where (1) $p$ is a pseudo-polynomial with $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(p)$ and $v(p) \asymp v(b)$, or $p = 1$; (2) $m$ is a pseudo-monomial with $v(m) \asymp v(b)$, or $m = 1$; (3) each $c_i$ is almost irreducible with infinite pseudo-support and $v(c_i) \asymp v(b)$." — p. 69 (plus uniqueness clauses (a), (b)).

**A.13.8  $\mathrm{R}(b)$.** "**Definition 8.4.2.** Given $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$, let $\mathrm{R}(b) := (\rho_\sigma(b) : \sigma \in \mathbf{G}_{/\asymp} \text{ such that } \operatorname{supp}(b) \cap \sigma \neq \varnothing)$." — p. 71 (upright R, PDF verified). Finite-product identity p. 70: "$b = T_{\sigma_1}(b) = \frac{T_{\sigma_1}(b)}{\tau_{\sigma_1}(b)}\tau_{\sigma_1}(b) = \rho_{\sigma_1}(b)T_{\sigma_2}(b) = \cdots = \rho_{\sigma_1}(b) \cdot \dots \cdot \rho_{\sigma_n}(b)$."

**A.13.9  infinite product $\dot{\prod}$ (two definitions).** "**Definition 8.4.4.** Let $(b_\sigma : \sigma \in J)$ be some family of non-zero reduced series, where $J$ is a non-empty subset of $\mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$. We define $\dot{\prod}_{\sigma \in J} b_\sigma$ to be any arbitrarily chosen $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$, if one exists, such that $\mathrm{R}(b) = (b_\sigma : \sigma \in J)$." — p. 72 (symbol: product sign with a dot above, PDF verified). "In other words, $\dot{\prod}$ is a section of $\mathrm{R}$ over its image." "**Definition 8.4.7.** Let $(b_i : i \in I)$ be some family of reduced series, where $I$ is some set. We write $\dot{\prod}_{i \in I} b_i = b$ when: for every $\sigma \in \mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$, the class $\{i \in I : v(b_i) \in \sigma\}$ is finite; if we let $J = \{[v(b_i)] : i \in I\}$ and $c_\sigma = \prod_{v(b_i) \in \sigma} b_i$, then $b = \dot{\prod}_{\sigma \in J} c_\sigma$ as per Definition 8.4.4." — p. 73. Named "a partially defined infinite product operator" (p. 6), "an appropriate infinite product of (some) omnific integers" (p. 5), "a suitable notion of infinite product" (abstract). Remark 8.4.5: non-uniqueness when $J$ has no minimum; canonical choice "in $\mathbf{No}$, we may then require that $r = 1$ and that $x$ is minimal with respect to simplicity." Remark 8.4.6 necessary conditions "(1) $v(b_\sigma) \in \sigma$ for all $\sigma \in J$; (2) $J$ is reverse well ordered; (3) if $\sigma \in J$ is not the minimum of $J$, then $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(b_\sigma)$." The usual surreal infinite product is "$\prod_{i \in I} b_i := \exp\left(\sum_{i \in I} \log(b_i)\right)$" (Remark 8.4.10, p. 75), called "the natural product" / "natural infinite product" of surreals (p. 76) — not to be confused with Hessenberg's natural product $\odot$.

**A.13.10  summable.** "This is only defined when the family $(\log(b_i) : i \in I)$ is *summable*, that is to say, the union of the supports $\operatorname{supp}(\log(b_i))$ is well ordered and each of its monomials appears in $\operatorname{supp}(\log(b_i))$ for at most finitely many $i \in I$." — p. 75, Remark 8.4.10.

**A.13.11  Theorem 8.4.8 (general factorisation).** "Let $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ be non-zero. Then there are a family $(c_i : i \in I)$ of reduced series $c_i \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ and a series $d \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$ such that $b = d \dot{\prod}_{i \in I} c_i$, where $\operatorname{supp}(d) \prec \operatorname{supp}(b)$ or $d = 1$, and for each $i \in I$, $v(c_i) \not\prec \operatorname{supp}(b)$ and $c_i$ is one of the following: (1) an irreducible pseudo-polynomial with $\langle\overline{\operatorname{supp}}(c_i)\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ of dimension $\geq 2$; (2) a pseudo-polynomial with $\langle\overline{\operatorname{supp}}(c_i)\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$ of dimension $1$ and $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(c_i)$; (3) a pseudo-monomial; (4) an almost irreducible series with infinite pseudo-support." — pp. 73–74, plus clauses (a)–(f). **Theorem C** (p. 6): "Let $b \in \mathbf{Oz}$ with $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(b)$. Then there exist a family $(c_i : i \in I)$ of reduced omnific integers such that $b = \dot{\prod}_{i\in I} c_i$ and each $c_i$ is irreducible, a pseudo-polynomial, or $\pm 1$."

### A.14 §8.5 The $\sup$ valuation on general $\mathbf{G}$

**A.14.1  $L(A)$, $U(A)$, $A^\#$; restricted Dedekind–MacNeille completion $\overline{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa$; $\oplus$, $x^\#$.** "**Definition 8.5.1.** Given a set $A \subseteq \mathbf{G}$, let $L(A) := \{x \in \mathbf{G} : x \leq A\}$, $U(A) := \{x \in \mathbf{G} : x \geq A\}$, and $A^\# := L(U(A)) \supseteq A$. We call **restricted Dedekind-MacNeille completion** of $\mathbf{G}$ the class $\overline{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa := \{A^\# : A \subseteq \mathbf{G}\} = \{L(A) : A \subseteq \mathbf{G},\ A \text{ set}, |A| < \kappa\}$ (where $|A| < \mathbf{On}$ is vacuously true). Given $\eta, \zeta \in \overline{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa$, $x \in \mathbf{G}$, we define the following: $\eta \leq \zeta \overset{\text{def}}{\Longleftrightarrow} \eta \subseteq \zeta, \quad \eta \oplus \zeta := (\eta + \zeta)^\#, \quad x^\# := \{x\}^\#$." — p. 77. Preamble: "The notation is similar to the one of [3, §XIII.13], but we use $\oplus$ for the sum in the completion of $\mathbf{G}$ to avoid ambiguities; also, we tweak the definition of completion so that it works when $\mathbf{G}$ is a proper class. Note that here *we do not assume that $\mathbf{G}$ is divisible*." — p. 77.

**A.14.2  $\sim$ on $\overline{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa^{\le 0}$ (second use of $\sim$).** "**Definition 8.5.3.** For $\eta, \zeta \in \overline{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa^{\leq 0}$, write $\eta \sim \zeta$ if for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $n\eta \leq x, y \leq n\zeta$ (or $n\zeta \leq x, y \leq n\eta$) we have $x - y \prec y$." — p. 77.

**A.14.3  $\sup(b)$ (class-valued); $\tilde{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa^{\le 0}$.** "**Definition 8.5.5.** Given $b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\leq 0}))_\kappa$, let $\sup(b)$ be the $\sim$-equivalence class of $\operatorname{supp}(b)^\#$. Let $\tilde{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa^{\leq 0}$ be the quotient of $\overline{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa^{\leq 0}$ by $\sim$." — p. 78. "**Proposition 8.5.6.** The map $\sup : \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{\leq 0}))_\kappa \to \tilde{\mathbf{G}}_\kappa^{\leq 0}$ is a multiplicative valuation." Also "$\pi_\sigma$ be the projection from $\mathbf{G}_{\preceq\sigma}$ to $H_\sigma$" (p. 78).

### A.15 §9 Primal series

**A.15.1  Conditions (A1)$_\sigma$, (A2)$_\sigma$, (A3).** "**Theorem 9.0.1.** Suppose that $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ is a pre-Schreier domain. Then the following conditions hold for all $\sigma \in \mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$: (A1)$_\sigma$ $H_\sigma \cong \mathbb{R}$ or $\sigma = [0]$; (A2)$_\sigma$ $\mathbf{G}_{\prec\sigma}$ has cofinality $\geq \kappa$, or $\mathbf{G}_{\prec\sigma} = \{0\}$ and $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{K}$, or $\sigma = [0]$; (A3) $\mathbf{Z}$ is a pre-Schreier domain. Moreover, $\mathbf{L}_\sigma((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ is a pre-Schreier domain for every $\sigma \in \mathbf{G}_{/\asymp}$." — p. 79. "The above three conditions are satisfied by $\mathbf{Oz}$, for every $\sigma$".

**A.15.2  $t^H(\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((H^{<0})))$; the Question.** "$t^H\left(\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((H^{<0}))\right) := \{t^x b : x \in H,\ b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((H^{<0}))\} = \{b \in \mathbf{K}((H)) : \operatorname{supp}(b) \text{ is bounded}\} = t^H \mathbf{K}((H^{\leq 0}))$." — p. 80. "**Question.** Let $H$ be a divisible Archimedean group. Is $t^H \mathbf{K}((H^{\leq 0}))$ a pre-Schreier domain?" — p. 80. (Also p. 6: "one could still ask whether the subring of $\mathbf{K}((H))$ of the series with support bounded from above is pre-Schreier.")

**A.15.3  Transfer lemma (Lemma 9.2.1) and Prop. 9.2.2.** "Let $\mathbf{L}$ be a field and $\mathbf{A}$ be a commutative $\mathbf{L}$-algebra equipped with an $\mathbf{L}$-algebra morphism $\pi : \mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{L}$. Let $\mathbf{S} \subseteq \mathbf{L}$ be a subring. An element $b \in \pi^{-1}(\mathbf{S})$ is primal in $\pi^{-1}(\mathbf{S})$ if and only if: (1) either $\pi(b) \neq 0$ is primal in $\mathbf{S}$ and $b$ is primal in $\mathbf{A}$, (2) or $\pi(b) = 0$ and $b$ is primal in $\pi^{-1}(\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbf{S}))$." — p. 81 ("an abstract transfer lemma"). "**Proposition 9.2.2.** Let $b \in \mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ be reduced and $\sigma = [v(b)]$, with $b \notin \mathbf{Z}$. Assume that $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(b)$, or that (A2)$_\sigma$ holds. Then $b$ is primal in $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ if and only if $\iota_\sigma(b)$ is primal in $\mathbf{L}((H_\sigma^{\leq 0}))$." — p. 82. Called "the primality transfer result" (p. 81), "a suitable primality transfer result (Proposition 8.2.9, inspired by [4, Cor. 4.3])" (p. 5), "lifts to a primal or prime series" (p. 84). Heading "9.2. Lifting primal elements". Verb for this: "transfer", "lift".

**A.15.4  Final examples.** "the omnific integer $\sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \omega^{1/(n+1)}$ is primal in $\mathbf{Oz}$, but obviously not prime as it is not irreducible." — p. 84. "**Corollary 9.2.7.** If $b \in \mathbf{Oz}$ is reduced with pseudo-support of order type $\omega \hat{+} k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{N}$, then $b$ is primal, and it is prime if and only if $0 \in \operatorname{supp}(b)$." — p. 84.

## §F. Where the paper disagrees with, corrects, or departs from another paper's usage

**F.1  "multiplicative" orientation of valuations (departure from standard valuation theory).** "we use the word 'multiplicative' to emphasise that the ultrametric inequality uses the maximum, rather than the minimum which is more common in valuation theory" — p. 7, §1.5.

**F.2  Hatted classical ordinal operations (departure from the literature's $+$, $\cdot$).** "In the literature, the classical operations are usually denoted by $+$ and $\cdot$, but to prevent confusion with the field operations on the surreal numbers, we decorate them with a hat." — p. 12. "To dispel any potential ambiguity, we also use $\hat{+}$, $\hat{\cdot}$ for the classical (non-commutative) ordinal operations." — p. 7.

**F.3  New function $\deg$ in place of Berarducci's $v_J$.** "In this paper, we use a different function $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) \to \mathbf{On} \cup \{-\infty\}$ that also happens to be a valuation." — p. 7. "Thus the degree is quite similar to $v_J$, with $\oplus$ replacing $\odot$, and $-\infty$ replacing 0. A key difference is that $v_J$ does not satisfy condition (3): the set $\{b \in \mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) : v_J(b) = 0\}$ is the ideal generated by $t^x$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}^{<0}$." — p. 7.

**F.4  Own earlier notation [15] abandoned.** "An earlier proof of condition (2) can be found in [15, Rem. 4.3, Cor. 4.7] from the authors of this paper, however with a considerably different notation based on a combination of tools from [2]. Here we shall describe a more self-contained proof based only on [2, Cor. 9.9]." — p. 7.

**F.5  Pitteloud's arguments translated.** "We reuse some of the arguments in [18], so we first translate them in our language." — p. 57.

**F.6  Gonshor's theorem re-scoped.** "Even though Gonshor's proof of the above statement is written for $\mathbf{No}$, it applies to any $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ without modifications." — p. 62.

**F.7  Pitteloud [19] reproved more generally / more shortly.** "The above observation yields a rather short and direct proof of the main theorem of [19] (where the group $\mathbf{G}$ is not assumed to be divisible)." — p. 63.

**F.8  Ritt's theorem rephrased; a case claimed missing.** "after rephrasing [22] to talk about series with finite support rather than exponential polynomials." — p. 21. "The case of $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{G}^{\le 0})$, even just for $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{G}$ sets, is slightly different and it seems to be missing from the literature." — p. 21.

**F.9  Birkhoff's completion notation modified.** "The notation is similar to the one of [3, §XIII.13], but we use $\oplus$ for the sum in the completion of $\mathbf{G}$ to avoid ambiguities; also, we tweak the definition of completion so that it works when $\mathbf{G}$ is a proper class." — p. 77.

**F.10  Own infinite product distinguished from the surreal one.** "note that it differs from the usual infinite product of surreal numbers, see Remark 8.4.10" — p. 6. "We shall see that $\prod$ and $\dot{\prod}$ disagree in general." — p. 75. "A minor tweak shows that the two products may even disagree on their common domain when infinite families are involved" — p. 76.

**F.11  Ribenboim-school $R((S))$ results declined as inapplicable.** "However, the rest of [21] and subsequent works on $R((S))$ (such as [13,14,26,5]) do not give information about $\mathbf{Oz}$: they typically restrict the order of $S$ in a way that exclude the monoid $G^{\leq 0}$." — p. 4.

**F.12  Zafrullah's original definition preferred to the refinement-property form.** "An integral domain is pre-Schreier if and only if every element is primal (this is actually the original definition from [25])." — p. 20.

**F.13  Correction to own earlier statement.** "We thank Pietro Freni for noticing an error in an earlier version of the statement below." — p. 19 (before Prop. 2.4.5).

**F.14  "Hahn field" terminology extended.** "We extend this terminology unchanged to respectively $\mathbf{K}((G))$ and $\mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}))_\kappa$." — p. 10.

**F.15  Reference to other weakened notions.** "We may recover a factorisation theorem by weakening the notion of irreducibility" (p. 52); "We handle this with a suitable weakening of the notion of irreducibility." (p. 67).

**G.4  Structural notes for later readers.**
- The paper is long (85 pp.) and has two registers: a general theory for $\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{K}((\mathbf{G}^{<0}))_\kappa$ (bold, class-sized) and the concrete case $\mathbf{Oz}$. Vocabulary that is specific to $\mathbf{Oz}$/general rings (reduced, pseudo-*, $T_\sigma$, $\tau_\sigma$, $\rho_\sigma$, $\mathrm{R}$, $\dot\prod$) lives in §8–§9; vocabulary for $\mathbf{K}((\mathbb{R}^{\le0}))$ (degree, principal, normal form, RV, $\mathrm{P}$, $\mathrm{p}(b)$) in §3–§7.
- Letter conventions worth copying: $b, c, d$ generic series; $p, q$ finite-support series; $B, C, D$ RV-elements; $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ ordinals; $x, y, z$ exponents; $k$ field element; $\sigma$ Archimedean class; $\kappa$ the cardinal bound; $H$ an Archimedean group; $\mathbf{L}$ a (class) field of coefficients after reduction; $\mathbf{S}$ its integer-part-like subring.
