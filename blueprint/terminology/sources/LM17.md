### 0.4 Standing hypotheses / the ambient ring [DEFINES — conventions]
- The Introduction works with general $K$ and $G$: "If $K$ is a field and $G$ an additive abelian ordered group" (L94). From Theorem 1.1 onward and in the whole body (Sections 2–5) the group is **fixed to $\mathbb{R}$** and the ring is **$K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$** (74 occurrences of `\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}`, 3 of `\mathbb{R}^{\leq0}`). The body never says "let $G=\mathbb{R}$"; it just writes $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ from L148 onward. The one place $G$ reappears inside the body is in Definition 2.1 (L414), "monomials $\{ t^\gamma : \gamma \in G^{<0} \}$", which is a slip for $\mathbb{R}^{<0}$ (flagged in §7).
- $K$ is "a field" (L94); no further hypothesis on $K$ is used in the body. The abstract says characteristic 0 (L75–76).
- The indeterminate is **$t$**; a series is $a = \sum_\gamma a_\gamma t^\gamma$ (L97). Exponents are Greek ($\gamma, \delta, \varepsilon, \beta$); coefficients are $a_\gamma \in K$; series are lower-case Latin $a, b, c, d, e, j$; germs are upper-case Latin $A, B, C, D, E$ in the one proof that distinguishes them (L568–594); ordinals are Greek $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \eta$ (so $\gamma$ serves both as an exponent in $\mathbb{R}$ and as an ordinal in §2.1).
- **Sign convention for the order.** "When $K$ is ordered, then $K((G))$ has a natural order as well, obtained by stipulating that $0 < t^\gamma < a$ for any $\gamma \in G^{>0}$ and for any positive element $a$ of the field $K$." (L105–108). So $t^\gamma$ with **positive** exponent is infinitesimal, and $K((G^{\leq 0}))$ — "the generalised power series with non-positive exponents" (L79–80) — is the ring of series with no infinitesimal part. The intro (L119–122): "$K((G^{< 0}))$ is the subring of the series with the support contained in the negative part $G^{< 0}$ of the group $G$."
- Superscript notation for sub-semigroups: $G^{\leq 0}$ (6×), $G^{< 0}$ (6×), $G^{<0}$ (2×), $G^{>0}$ (L107), $\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$ (74×), $\mathbb{R}^{< 0}$ (4×), $\mathbb{R}^{<0}$ (5×). The ring notation is always $K((\cdot))$ with double parentheses.
- Non-zero constants are written **$K^*$** (3×: L193, L663, L805), used as "constants $k_i \in K^*$" (L193) and as the units ("$b \in K^*$, hence ... $b$ is a unit", L805–806).
- The natural numbers are $\mathbb{N}$ (L964 onward), used as an index set; finite ordinals are "identified with the natural numbers $0$, $1$, $2$, $\ldots$" (L335–336) so **$0 \in \mathbb{N}$**.

## 1. Generalised power series — the objects (Introduction, L92–145)

### 1.1 formal series, coefficients, exponents [ASSUMES/DEFINES — defined in passing]
- L94–98: "If $K$ is a field and $G$ an additive abelian ordered group, a \emph{formal series} with \emph{coefficients} in $K$ and \emph{exponents} in $G$ is a formal sum $a = \sum_\gamma a_\gamma t^\gamma$, where $a_\gamma \in K$ and $\gamma \in G$."
- "formal series" used 2× (L95, L99); thereafter only "series" (74 occurrences of the word, mostly as "series" alone) or "generalised power series"/"power series".
- The paper says "additive abelian ordered group" (L94) and "ordered abelian group" (L76); both orders of adjectives occur.

### 1.2 support, $S_a$ [DEFINES]
- L98–99: "We call \emph{support} of $a$ the set $S_a := \{\gamma \in G : a_\gamma \neq 0\}$."
- Symbol: $S_a$ (subscripted series name); also $S_{a_{i+1}}$, $S_{t^{\gamma_{i+1}}a_{i+1}}$ (L975, L986).
- **Convention: inequalities between a set and a point.** "$S_a \leq \gamma$" (L422) means every element of $S_a$ is $\leq \gamma$; "$S_{a_{i + 1}} > \gamma_i - \gamma_{i + 1}$" (L975) likewise. Not explained anywhere; used as native.
- Phrasing: "with the support contained in the negative part $G^{<0}$" (L121); "series with finite support" (L282); "pairwise disjoint supports" (L1000); "the support of the series $t^{\gamma_{i}}a_{i}$ do not overlap" (L979–980).

### 1.3 generalised power series, $K((G))$ [DEFINES (with citation to Hahn)]
- L99–103: "A formal series $a$ is said to be a \emph{generalised power series} if its support $S_a$ is well-ordered. The collection of all generalised power series, denoted by $K((G))$, is a field with respect to the obvious operations $+$ and $\cdot$ defined for ordinary power series (see~\cite{Hahn1995})."
- Names used for the elements: "generalised power series" (5×), "power series" (L139 "a power series $a \in K((G^{\leq 0}))$"; L265 "germ-like power series"), "series" (dominant, dozens), "formal series" (2×, intro only). The abstract glosses: "generalised power series (i.e., formal sums with well-ordered support)" (L74–75).
- Attribution: Hahn [Hahn1995 = reprint of "Über die nichtarchimedischen Größensysteme"] for the field structure (L103). Gleyzal 1937 for "any ordered field can be represented as a subfield of some $\mathbb{R}((G))$" (L109–111).
- $K((G))$ appears 7× (intro only); body uses $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$.

### 1.4 $K((G^{\leq 0}))$, $K((G^{<0}))$, integer part [REUSES]
- L78–80 (abstract): "the subring $K((G^{\leq 0}))$ of $K((G))$ consisting of the generalised power series with non-positive exponents."
- L119–122: "$\mathbb{Z} + K((G^{< 0}))$ is an integer part of $K((G))$, where $K((G^{< 0}))$ is the subring of the series with the support contained in the negative part $G^{< 0}$ of the group $G$."
- L126: "$K + K((G^{< 0})) = K((G^{\leq 0}))$".
- Integer part, defined inline with attribution to Mourgues–Ressayre: L114–119 "every real closed field $R$ has an integer part (i.e., a subring $Z$ such that for all $x \in R$ there exists a unique integer part $\lfloor x \rfloor \in Z$ of $x$ such that $\lfloor x \rfloor \leq x < \lfloor x \rfloor + 1$)~\cite{Mourgues1993}". Note "integer part" is used both for the subring $Z$ and for the element $\lfloor x \rfloor$.
- Phrasing about arithmetic: "The ring $\mathbb{Z} + K((G^{< 0}))$ has a non-trivial arithmetic behaviour, some of which is already visible in $K + K((G^{< 0})) = K((G^{\leq 0}))$." (L124–126). "When $G$ is divisible, the ring $K((G^{\leq 0}))$ is non-noetherian, as for instance we have $t^{-1} = t^{-\frac{1}{2}} \cdot t^{-\frac{1}{2}} = \ldots$" (L126–130).

### 1.5 omnific integers, surreal numbers [ASSUMES]
- L133–136: "in fact, his result implies that $1 + \sum_{n} t^{-\frac{1}{n}}$ is irreducible in the ring of omnific integers, the natural integer part of surreal numbers, which are also of the form $\mathbb{Z} + \mathbb{R}((G^{< 0}))$ for a suitable $G$." Not defined; Conway [Conway1976] is cited for the question, not for these terms. Meaning attached is my inference.

### 1.6 Berarducci's example and Conway's question [REUSES]
- L130–133: "Berarducci~\cite{Berarducci2000} proved that $K((G^{\leq 0}))$, when $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq G$, contains irreducible series, such as $1 + \sum_{n} t^{-\frac{1}{n}}$, answering a question of Conway \cite{Conway1976}".
- Phrasing: "answering a question of Conway"; "A fundamental result of Berarducci ensures the existence of irreducible series" (L77–78).

## 2. Order type, the ideal $J$, germs (Introduction and §2.2)

### 2.1 order type, $\ot(a)$ [DEFINES — the notation; the notion is GENERIC]
- Intro, L138–140: "let the \emph{order type} $\ot(a)$ of a power series $a \in K((G^{\leq 0}))$ be the ordinal number representing the order type of its support $S_a$."
- §2.2, L395–398: "Given $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, we let $\ot(a)$ be the order type of support $S_a$ of $a$ (recall that the support of $a$ is a well-ordered subset of $\mathbb{R}$, hence $\ot(a)$ is a countable ordinal)."
- Symbol $\ot$ (upright), 36 occurrences. Always applied to a series, never to a set directly (the order type of a set is spoken of in words, L139–140, L296).
- Basic inequalities stated as "One can verify that" (L398–404): "$\ot(a + b) \leq \ot(a) \oplus \ot(b)$; $\ot(a \cdot b) \leq \ot(a) \odot \ot(b)$." Followed by "However, the above inequalities may well be strict for certain values of $a$ and $b$." (L406–407).
- Phrasing: "has order type $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$" (L150); "series ... of order type $\omega$ or $\omega + 1$ and ordinal-value $\omega$" (L213–215, L523–524); "a sequence of series of order type $\omega^\alpha$" (L964–965).

### 2.2 the ideal $J$ [REUSES from Berarducci; two definitions given]
- Intro, L140–145: "let $J$ be the ideal of the series that are divisible by $t^\gamma$ for some $\gamma \in G^{<0}$ (as noted before for $\gamma = -1$, such series cannot be factored into irreducibles when $G$ is divisible, since $t^\gamma = t^{\frac{\gamma}{2}} t^{\frac{\gamma}{2}} = \ldots$)."
- Definition 2.1 (L412–417): "We let $J$ be the ideal of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ generated by the set of monomials $ \{ t^\gamma : \gamma \in G^{<0} \}$." [$G$ here is a slip for $\mathbb{R}$; flagged §7.]
- Remark 2.2 (L419–425): "The ideal $J$ can also be defined by looking at the support: for every series $a$, $a \in J$ if and only if there exists $\gamma < 0$ such that $S_a \leq \gamma$. In particular, $a+J = b+J$ if and only if there exists $\gamma < 0$ such that for all $\delta \geq \gamma$, the coefficients of $t^\delta$ in $a$ and $b$ coincide."
- The abstract's wording: "the ideal generated by the non-constant monomials" (L84–85).
- Symbol: always $J$ (no decoration). The sum $J + K$ (19× with spaces, 6× as `J+K`) is used as a $K$-vector space; "$J_{\omega^1} = J + K$" (L541) and "$J_\omega = J + K$" (L880–881).
- "monomials" 2× (L85, L414); "$t^\gamma$" is the monomial; "non-constant monomials".
- Theorem 1.1 hypothesis phrasing: "$a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) \setminus J$ (equivalently, $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ not divisible by $t^\gamma$ for any $\gamma < 0$)" (L148–150).
- Primality of $J$: Proposition 2.7 [Berarducci Thm 9.8] "The ideal $J$ of $ K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ is prime." (L481–483), with a two-line proof from the multiplicative property (L484–489) — "Note that $a \in J$ if and only if $\vj(a)=0$." For general $G$: "in fact, $J$ is prime for arbitrary choices of $G$, see~\cite{Pitteloud2002}; for further extensions to arbitrary groups $G$, see~\cite{Biljakovic2006}" (L180–182).

### 2.3 germ; the quotient ring of germs $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))/J$ [DEFINES — the word "germ" is this paper's; see note]
- Definition 2.1 (L414–416): "For every $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, we call the \emph{germ} of $a$ the coset $a+J \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq0}))/J$."
- Intro, L177–179: "the ideal $J$ is prime, so the quotient ring of \emph{germs} $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) / J$ is an integral domain".
- The paper does **not** attribute the word "germ" to anyone; it introduces it itself with `\emph{}` at L178 and L415. Whether it is inherited cannot be determined from this paper.
- Counts: "germ" 69 occurrences including "germ-like" (39); "germs" 16; "germ of" 1; "germs of" 4.
- Phrasings for germs: "Every non-zero germ in $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) / J$ admits a unique factorisation into irreducibles" (L208–209); "All germs in $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) / J$ of ordinal-value $\omega$ are prime" (L564–565); "the germs of ordinal-value at most $\omega^3$" (L219); "its germ $ab + t^{\gamma} + J = ab + J = (a + J)(b + J)$ is reducible" (L814–815); "If the germ $a + J$ of $a$ is irreducible in $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) / J$" (L796–797); "there exist series $c$ of ordinal-value $\omega^3$ such that $c + J$ is irreducible" (L1049–1050); "for any given germ $a + J$" (L869); "Let $A = a + J$, $B = b + J$, $C = c + J$ and $D = d + J$ be non-zero germs of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) / J$" (L568–569).
- A germ is always written as a coset "$a + J$"; in one proof germs get capital letters ($A, B, C, D, E, B', C'$; L568–594).
- Relationship germ/series, phrasing: "even if a series ... has an irreducible germ, it may well be reducible" (L621–622); "the results on germs cannot be lifted automatically to all series" (L623–624); "irreducible germs do lift to irreducible germ-like series" (L792–793); "there are some series which behave similarly enough to germs so that the same techniques can be applied to them" (L624–626); "Every non-zero germ in $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) / J$" (L208, L598).
- "germ of $a$ at $\gamma$" — see 2.9 (translated truncation).

### 2.4 the congruence notation $a \equiv b \mod V$ [DEFINES — Notation 2.3]
- Notation 2.3 (L427–432): "Let $V \subseteq K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ be any $K$-vector space. Then, let us write, for $a, \, b \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, $a \equiv b \mod V$ if $a-b \in V$."
- Used with $V = J$ (most), $J + K$ (L441, L896), $J_{\omega^\alpha}$, $J_{\omega^2}$, $J_{\omega^{\alpha+1}}$, $J_{\vj(ab)}$ etc. 27 `\equiv`, 33 `\mod`.
- **Convention:** the modulus is written *after* the whole equation, e.g. "$a \equiv b \mod J + K$" (L441) meaning modulo the space $J+K$ (no parentheses around $J+K$); in a display: "${(ab)}^{\vert\gamma} \equiv \sum \ldots \mod J$" (L515–516).
- **Convention (slip or deliberate):** in several places `=` is used where `≡` is meant: "$a^k b = c^l d \mod J_{\vj(a^{k}b)}$" (L557, inside Proposition 3.2 as quoted from Pitteloud); "$ab = cd \mod J_{\vj(ab)}$" (L576). Elsewhere `\equiv`. Flagged §7.
- The modulus $V$ is also used as a noun: "modulo the vector space $J+K$" (L1002), "modulo $J + K$" (L845, L861), "modulo the subspace $J + K$" (L851–852).

### 2.5 divisibility notation $a \vert c$; $b \vert a \mod J_{\omega^\alpha}$ [DEFINES]
- L549–551: "Pitteloud proved that either $a \vert c$ or $a \vert d$ in $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ (where $a \vert c$ means $a$ divides $c$)". Symbol is `\vert` (vertical bar), used for germs too: "We claim that $A \vert C$ or $A \vert D$" (L570–571).
- Definition 3.1 second half (L535–538): "Moreover, we write $b \vert a \mod J_{\omega^\alpha}$ if there exists $c \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ such that $a \equiv bc \mod J_{\omega^\alpha}$."
- Usage: "either $a \vert c \mod J_{\vj(c)}$ or $a \vert d \mod J_{\vj(d)}$" (L558, L576–577). Unpacked as: "Then there are $e,c' \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ such that $c = ae + c'$ with $\vj(c') < \vj(c)$" (L580–581).
- Word "divides" once (L551); "divisible by $t^\gamma$" (L141, L149); "$B$ must divide one of the factors" (L612–613).

### 2.6 ordinal-value $v_J$ [REUSES from Berarducci — Def. 5.2 — unchanged name and symbol]
- Intro, Definition 1.2, headed `[{\cite[Def.\ 5.2]{Berarducci2000}}]` (L157–166): "For $a \in K((G^{\leq 0}))$, the \emph{ordinal-value} $\vj(a)$ of $a$ is: (1) if $a \in J$, then $\vj(a):=0$; (2) if $a \in J+K$ and $a \notin J$, then $\vj(a):=1$; (3) if $a \notin J+K$, then $\vj(a) := \min \{\ot(a') \,:\, a-a' \in J+K\}$."
- §2.2 restatement, Definition 2.4 (L434–445), preceded by (L392–393) "We now recall the definition and the basic properties of the ordinal-value map introduced by Berarducci in~\cite{Berarducci2000}." and (L407–410) "In order to get a better algebraic behaviour, Berarducci introduced the so called \emph{ordinal-value} $\vj : K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) \rightarrow \on$ by considering only the `tail' of the support." Definition 2.4 verbatim: "The \emph{ordinal-value} $\vj : K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) \rightarrow \on$ is defined by: $\vj (a) := 0$ if $a \in J$; $1$ if $a \not\in J$ and $a \in J + K$; $\min \{ \ot(b) : b \equiv a \, \mod \, J + K \}$ otherwise." (displayed as a three-case brace, L437–444).
- Symbol: $v_J$ (`\vj`, 130 occurrences — by far the most used symbol of the paper). Target: $\mathbf{On}$. Described as "a function resembling a valuation but taking values into ordinal numbers" (L154–155) and as "the ordinal-value map" (L393, L278).
- Remark 2.5, `rem:vj-cnf` (L447–455): "Suppose that $a$ is not in $J+K$ and write $\ot(a) = \omega^{\alpha_1} + \ldots + \omega^{\alpha_n}$ in Cantor normal form. Then $\vj(a)$ is precisely the last infinite term of the Cantor normal form of $\ot(a)$ (which is either $\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}}$ or $\omega^{\alpha_n}$, depending on whether $0 \in S_a$ or $0 \notin S_a$). Note in particular that the ordinal-value takes only values of the form $\omega^\alpha$ or $0$."
- Induced map on germs (L457–462): "Furthermore, since $a \equiv b \mod J$ implies $\vj(a) = \vj(b)$, the map $\vj$ induces an analogous ordinal-value $\overline{v}_J : K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) / J \rightarrow \on$ by defining $\overline{v}_J(a + J) := \vj(a)$. With a slight abuse of notation, we will use the symbol $\vj$ for both $\vj$ and $\overline{v}_J$." — **Convention:** $\overline{v}_J$ is introduced and then immediately dropped; everywhere after, $\vj(A)$ is written for germs $A$ (L570, L591, L602, L605).
- Phrasing for ordinal-value: "of ordinal-value $\omega$" (dominant; e.g. L215, L218, L223, L564); "has ordinal-value" only once: "The product of the remaining factors has either ordinal-value $\omega^2$ or $\omega$" (L614–615); "ordinal-value at most $\omega^3$" (L219, L244); "ordinal-value $\leq \omega^3$" (L225, L249, L599, L773); "germs of ordinal-value $\omega$ are prime" (L217–218); "a series ... of ordinal-value $\omega^{\alpha+1}$" (L904).
- "tail" (in single quotes at L410; "the tail of $c^{\vert\gamma}$ is a proper (translated) truncation of the tail of $c$", L834–836) is the informal word for the part of the support near $0$ that the ordinal-value sees. The complementary "first term of the Cantor normal form" is called neither "head" nor anything else in the text; only the *label* `cor:head-ordinal-value` (L749) uses "head".

- Proposition 2.6, headed `[{\cite[Lem.\ 5.5 and Thm.\ 9.7]{Berarducci2000}}]` (L467–475): "Let $a, b \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$. Then: (1) $\vj(a + b) \leq \, \max\{\vj(a), \vj(b)\}$, with equality if $\vj(a) \neq \vj(b)$, (2) $\vj(ab) = \vj(a) \odot \vj(b)$ \emph{(multiplicative property)}."
- The name **"multiplicative property"** (4×: L472–473, L477, L543, L766, L780, L803–804) is fixed here by the italic parenthetical; invoked as "By the multiplicative property, ..." (L766, L780, L803) and "By the multiplicative property, one can easily verify that ..." (L542–543).
- L477–478: "The multiplicative property is the crucial ingredient that leads to the main results in~\cite{Berarducci2000}."

### 2.8 Berarducci's irreducibility theorem (Theorem 1.1) [REUSES — Berarducci Thm 10.5]

### 3.2 refinement; common refinement; Conway's conjecture [DEFINES "refinement"; REUSES the conjecture from Conway]
- L184–194: "If $a = b_1 \cdot \ldots \cdot b_n$ is a factorisation of a series $a$, possibly with some reducible factors, a \emph{refinement} is another factorisation of $a$ obtained by replacing each $b_i$ with a further factorisation of $b_i$. ## 4. Germ-like series and the critical point (§4, L619–816)

### 4.1 germ-like [DEFINES — the paper's central new term]
- Intro, L229–232: "Moreover, we shall isolate the notion of \emph{germ-like} series: we say that $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ is germ-like if either $\ot(a) = \vj(a)$, or $\vj(a) > 1$ and $\ot(a) = \vj(a) + 1$ (see \prettyref{def:germ-like})."
- Definition 4.1, label `def:germ-like` (L628–632): "We say that an $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ is \emph{germ-like} if either $\ot(a) = \vj(a)$ or $\vj(a) > 1$ and $\ot(a) = \vj(a) + 1$."
- Motivation (L621–626): "Unfortunately, even if a series in ${K((\mathbb{R}))}^{\leq 0}$ has an irreducible germ, it may well be reducible (see for instance $(t^{-1} -1) (1 + \sum_{n} t^{-\frac{1}{n}})$). This implies that the results on germs cannot be lifted automatically to all series. On the other hand, there are some series which behave similarly enough to germs so that the same techniques can be applied to them." [`${K((\mathbb{R}))}^{\leq 0}$` is a typo for $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$; flagged §7.]
- Usage: adjective only ("germ-like series", "germ-like power series" L265, "is germ-like", "$b$ and $c$ are germ-like"); never a noun. 39 occurrences. "non-zero germ-like series" (L247, L248, L271, L754, L772).
- Characterisation, Lemma 4.4, label `lem:germ-like-crit` (L654–657): "If $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, $a$ is germ-like if and only if $\crit(a) = 0$."
- Proof phrasing (L668–671): "Therefore, $\ot(a) = \vj(a)$ holds if and only if the Cantor normal form of $\ot(a)$ is $\omega^\alpha$ for some $\alpha \in \on$. Similarly, $\vj(a) = \ot(a) + 1$ holds if and only if the Cantor normal form of $\ot(a)$ is $\omega^{\alpha} + 1$ for some $\alpha \in \on$." [second equation reversed in source; flagged §7.]
- Edge cases, as the paper states them (L659–663): "if $a \in J$, then $\vj(a) = 0$; in this case, we just note that $\ot(a) = 0$ if and only if $a = 0$ ... if $a \in J + K$ but $a \notin J$, then $\vj(a) = 1$, and clearly $\ot(a) = 1$ if and only if $a \in K^*$". So: the germ-like series of ordinal-value $0$ is exactly $0$; those of ordinal-value $1$ are exactly $K^*$; consequently the clause "$\vj(a) > 1$" in the definition excludes $k + t^\gamma$ type series (inference from the definition).
- Recurrent deduction idiom: "If $\vj(b) = 1$, then $\ot(b) = 1$, hence $b \in K$" (L783); "Since $b$ is germ-like it follows that $\ot(b) = 1$, which implies that $b \in K^*$" (L804–805); "Since $b$ is germ-like, we have $\ot(b) = \omega$ or $\ot(b) = \omega + 1$" (L785–786).

## 6. Ordinal arithmetic (§2.1, L289–388) [GENERIC, with the paper's own fixed choices]

The paper calls this "a self-contained presentation of the classical and well-known properties of ordinal arithmetic" (L291–292). Everything here is generic; recorded because the paper *fixes* definitions and notation, some of which another paper might do differently.

- **order similar / order types** (L293–296): "Two (linearly) ordered sets $X$ and $Y$ are called order similar if they are isomorphic. The order similarity is an equivalence relation and its classes are called \emph{order types}."
- **ordinal number / well-ordered** (L298–300): "An \emph{ordinal number} is the order type of a \emph{well-ordered} set, i.e., an ordered set with the property that any non-empty subset has a minimum." — Convention: ordinals are order types (equivalence classes), not von Neumann sets.
- **order on ordinals** (L300–304): "Given two ordinal numbers $\alpha, \beta$, we say that $\alpha \leq \beta$ if there are two representatives $A$ and $B$ such that $A \subseteq B$ and such that the inclusion of $A$ in $B$ is a homomorphism; we say that $\alpha < \beta$ if $\alpha \leq \beta$ and $\alpha \neq \beta$." ("homomorphism" here = order-preserving map; the paper's word.)
- **$\mathbf{On}$** [ASSUMES — used, never defined] (L304–306): "A key observation in the theory of ordinals is that $\on$ itself is \emph{well-ordered by $\leq$}. This lets us define ordinal arithmetic by induction on $\leq$:". The symbol $\mathbf{On}$ (bold) for the class of all ordinals appears 14× (`\on`), first here without introduction. Also "$\vj : K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0})) \rightarrow \on$" (L409, L436), "$\alpha \in \on$" (L532, L669, L911).
- **zero, successor $S(\alpha)$, supremum $\sup(A)$** (L309–315): "the minimum ordinal in $\on$ is called \emph{zero} and is denoted by $0$; given an $\alpha \in \on$, the \emph{successor} $S(\alpha)$ of $\alpha$ is the minimum $\beta$ such that $\beta > \alpha$; given a set $A \subseteq \on$, the \emph{supremum} $\sup(A)$ is the minimum $\beta$ such that $\beta \geq \alpha$ for all $\alpha \in A$".
- **sum, product, exponentiation** (L316–324): "\emph{sum}: $\alpha + 0 := \alpha$, $\alpha + \beta := \sup_{\gamma < \beta} \{S(\alpha + \gamma)\}$; \emph{product}: $\alpha \cdot 0 := 0$, $\alpha \cdot 1 := \alpha$, $\alpha \cdot \beta := \sup_{\gamma < \beta} \{\alpha \cdot \gamma + \alpha\}$; \emph{exponentiation}: $\alpha^0 := 1$, $\alpha^\beta := \sup_{\gamma < \beta} \{\alpha^\gamma \cdot \alpha\}$." — Convention: ordinal product written with explicit `\cdot`; ordinal sum `+` (same glyphs as the ring operations; context disambiguates). Note these "definitions" are as written in the source (the sup-formulae as stated do not literally give successors/limits separately; recorded verbatim, not endorsed).
- **Properties** (L327–332): "One can easily verify that sum and product are associative, but not commutative, that the product is distributive over the sum in the second argument, and that $\alpha^{\beta + \gamma} = \alpha^\beta \cdot \alpha^\gamma$. Moreover, sum, product and exponentiation are strictly increasing and continuous in the second argument."
- **finite, natural numbers, limit, $\omega$** (L334–340): "The \emph{finite} ordinals are the ones that are represented by finite ordered sets. They can be identified with the natural numbers $0$, $1$, $2$, $\ldots$, where ordinal arithmetic coincides with Peano's arithmetic. The ordinals that are not zero or successors are called \emph{limit}, and one can verify that $\alpha$ is a limit if and only if $\alpha \neq 0$ and $\alpha = \sup_{\beta < \alpha} \{\beta\}$. The smallest limit ordinal is called $\omega$."
- **subtraction, division, logarithm** (L342–352): "The three operations admit notions of subtraction, division and logarithm. More precisely, given $\alpha \leq \beta$, there exist: a unique $\gamma$ such that $\alpha + \gamma = \beta$; unique $\gamma, \delta$ with $\delta < \alpha$ such that $\alpha \cdot \gamma + \delta = \beta$; unique $\gamma, \delta, \eta$ with $\delta < \beta$, $\eta < \alpha$ such that $\beta^{\gamma} \cdot \delta + \eta = \alpha$." [Third bullet has $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in roles inconsistent with $\alpha \leq \beta$ and with the first two bullets; as in source; flagged §7.]
- **Cantor normal form** (L354–361): "In particular, for all $\alpha \in \on$ there is a unique finite sequence $\beta_1 \geq \beta_2 \geq \ldots \geq \beta_n \geq 0$ such that $\alpha = \omega^{\beta_1} + \ldots + \omega^{\beta_n}$. The expression on the right-hand side is called \emph{Cantor normal form} of $\alpha$." — **Convention:** CNF with *repeated* exponents and no finite coefficients (each $\omega^{\beta_i}$ listed separately, $\beta_i$ weakly decreasing). The paper speaks of the "terms" of the CNF: "the last infinite term of the Cantor normal form of $\ot(a)$" (L450–451, L666), "the first term of the Cantor normal form" (L278, L647–648); "write $\ot(a) = \omega^{\alpha_1} + \ldots + \omega^{\alpha_n}$ in Cantor normal form" (L448–450).
- **CNF arithmetic rules** (L361–371): "if $\alpha < \beta$, $\omega^\alpha + \omega^\beta = \omega^\beta$; if $\alpha = \omega^{\beta_1} + \ldots + \omega^{\beta_n}$ is in Cantor normal form and $\gamma > 0$, then $\alpha \cdot \omega^\gamma = \omega^{\beta_1 + \gamma}$."
- **Hessenberg's natural sum $\oplus$ and natural product $\odot$** (L373–388): "Finally, we recall that $\on$ also admits different commutative operations called \emph{Hessenberg's natural sum $\oplus$ and natural product} $\odot$. These can be defined rather easily using the Cantor normal form. Given $ \alpha = \omega^{\gamma_1} + \omega^{\gamma_2} + \ldots + \omega^{\gamma_n}$ and $ \beta = \omega^{\gamma_{n+1}} + \omega^{\gamma_{n+2}} + \ldots + \omega^{\gamma_{n+m}}$ in Cantor normal form, the natural sum $\alpha \oplus \beta$ is defined as $\alpha \oplus \beta := \omega^{\gamma_{\pi(1)}} + \omega^{\gamma_{\pi(2)}} + \ldots + \omega^{\gamma_{\pi(n+m)}}$, where $\pi$ is a permutation of the integers $1, \ldots, n+m$ such that $\gamma_{\pi(1)} \geq \ldots \geq \gamma_{\pi(n+m)}$, and the natural product is defined by $\alpha \odot \beta := \bigoplus_{1 \leq i \leq n} \bigoplus_{n+1 \leq j \leq k+m} \omega^{\gamma_j \oplus \gamma_j}$." — Symbols $\oplus$, $\odot$ (`\oplus`, `\odot`; big version `\bigoplus`). Attribution: "Hessenberg's" (no bibliographic reference). The natural-product formula as typed has "$k+m$" (should be $n+m$) and "$\gamma_j \oplus \gamma_j$" (should be $\gamma_i \oplus \gamma_j$); flagged §7. Earlier (L173–174): "(where $\odot$ is Hessenberg's natural product on ordinal numbers)".
- **Ordinal exponent towers** used: $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$ (L150, L234), $\omega^3$, $\omega^2$, $\omega^{\alpha+1}$, $\omega + 1$.

And L264–266: "all the known results about irreducibility and primality of generalised power series are in fact about germ-like power series." — a re-description, not a correction.
2. **Restating Berarducci's ordinal-value with a different but equivalent clause.** Definition 1.2 (attributed to Berarducci Def. 5.2) uses "$\min \{\ot(a') : a-a' \in J+K\}$" (L164), while Definition 2.4 uses the paper's own congruence notation "$\min \{ \ot(b) : b \equiv a \, \mod \, J + K \}$" (L441). Same object, re-symbolled with Notation 2.3. Whether Berarducci's original wording matches either cannot be determined from this paper.
3. **Proposing an alternative ordinal-value.** L275–285: "we note that \prettyref{lem:crit-mult} and its following \prettyref{cor:head-ordinal-value} suggest an alternative multiplicative ordinal-value map whose value is the \emph{first} term of the Cantor normal form of the order type, rather than the last infinite one." — a departure from Berarducci's choice, announced for future work, not a correction.
4. **Abuse of notation declared.** L460–462: "With a slight abuse of notation, we will use the symbol $\vj$ for both $\vj$ and $\overline{v}_J$." — a self-declared convention, not a disagreement.
5. **Two definitions of $J$.** The intro's "ideal of the series that are divisible by $t^\gamma$ for some $\gamma \in G^{<0}$" (L141–142) versus Definition 2.1's "generated by the set of monomials" (L413–414) and Remark 2.2's support characterisation (L420–422); presented as equivalent, no source singled out.
6. **Positioning between two conjectures.** L266–268: its Conjecture 1.6 is "a reasonable intermediate statement between Conway's conjecture and Berarducci's conjecture" — a new conjecture, not a departure.
