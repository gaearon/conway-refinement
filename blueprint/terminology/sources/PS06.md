## Part A. What the paper defines: terms, notation, and the conventions fixed alongside them

### A.1 The coefficient field `K` — [DEF] (convention)

- General setting in §1: "Let $K$ be a field and $G$ an ordered abelian group." (§1, p.1277, l.13)
- Standing convention, fixed once: "In this paper, $K$ will always denote a field of characteristic zero." (§2, p.1279, l.62)
- Despite the standing convention, every numbered statement from Lemma 3.1 onward restates the hypothesis in full: "Let $K$ be a field of characteristic zero, $R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$, and $a \in R$." (Lemma 3.1, p.1281, l.108); "Let $K$ be a field of characteristic zero, let $R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ and let $a \in R$." (Cor. 3.3, l.194; Cor. 3.4, l.200; Thm 4.1, l.208). Theorems A and B likewise (l.39, l.43). Prop. 3.2 restates it without "$a \in R$" (l.132).
- Characteristic zero is stated as the hypothesis under which Berarducci's irreducibility results hold: "if $K$ is a field of characteristic zero and $G$ is an ordered abelian divisible group, then there exist irreducible elements in $K((G^{\leq 0}))$" (§1, p.1277, l.15); "When $K$ has characteristic zero, Berarducci [1] proved that ..." (§1, p.1278, l.29). Count: "characteristic zero" 14×.
- `K` is also treated as a subspace of `R`: "Now $K$ is a subspace of $R$ and thus so is $J + K$." (§2, p.1280, l.68). Elements of `K` are written $k, k', k_1, \dots, k_6, k_b, k_c$; the phrase is "for some $0 \neq k \in K$" (l.173), "there exist constants $k_b, k_c \in K$" (l.290).

### A.2 The exponent group `G`; the case `G = ℝ` — [DEF] (convention)

- "Let $K$ be a field and $G$ an ordered abelian group." (§1, p.1277, l.13). Word order "ordered abelian group" (2×); for Berarducci's hypothesis: "an ordered abelian divisible group" (l.15) — note the adjective order *ordered abelian divisible*, not "divisible ordered abelian".
- The paper's own setting: "for the case where $K$ is a field of characteristic zero and $G$ is the additive group of reals" (§1, p.1277, l.15); "for the case when $G = \mathbb{R}$ it is a countable ordinal" (§1, p.1278, l.29).
- Exponent variables: `γ` dominates in §1–2 ($\gamma \in G$, $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$, $t^\gamma$); `x, y, z` (reals) dominate in §3–4 ("$x < 0$", "$x \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$"); `α` once as a summation index ($a = \sum_\alpha a_\alpha t^\alpha$, l.33) and once as an element of the support ("there exists an element $\alpha \in \mathrm{Supp}(a)$", Cor. 3.4, l.200) — `α` is otherwise an ordinal variable; `β, ξ` in Prop. 2.1; `ν` once (l.85).
- The set of allowed exponents is written $\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$ (never "$\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}$" or "$(-\infty,0]$"): "$\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$" (l.33, l.70, l.74, l.85), "$x \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$" (l.94), "pairs $(\beta, \xi) \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0} \times \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$" (l.76).

### A.3 Formal sum, coefficients, exponents, the variable `t` — [DEF] (notation)

- "Given a formal sum $a = \sum_\gamma a_\gamma t^\gamma$ with coefficients $a_\gamma$ in $K$ and exponents $\gamma$ in $G$" (§1, p.1277, l.13). The indeterminate is `t`; exponents are written as superscripts $t^\gamma$; the coefficient of $t^\gamma$ in $a$ is $a_\gamma$ (subscript matching the series' letter: $b_\beta$, $b_x$, $c_x$, $d_x$, $e_x$, $a_{x_0}$, $b_{y_0}$, $c_{z_0}$).
- Re-introductions of the same convention: "for an element $a = \sum_\alpha a_\alpha t^\alpha \in R$" (l.33); "if $b = \sum_\beta b_\beta t^\beta \in R$" (l.70); "$b = \sum_x b_x t^x$ and $c = \sum_x c_x t^x$" (Prop. 3.2, l.132); "for $a = \sum_\gamma a_\gamma t^\gamma \in R$ and $\nu \in \mathbb{R}$" (l.85).
- "formal sum" 3×; "the part of the power series representing $a$ with exponents just to the left of zero" (§2, p.1279, l.62) — the series is spoken of as *representing* the element.
- Individual summands are called **terms**: "every term of the generalized power series $b$ and $c$ is a fixed linear combination of the terms of $d$ and $e$" (l.290); "$c$ and $kb$ differ in at most a finite number of terms" (l.173); "except possibly for the constant term" (l.290).
- **Constant term**: "the elements of $\mathbb{R}((G^{\leq 0}))$ with (rational) integer constant term" (l.48); "except possibly for the constant term" (l.290); the constant coefficient is written $a_0$, $b_0$: "$b - b_0 = a - a_0$" (l.190). Not defined; ordinary usage.
- **Monomial** (abstract only, 2×): "an element of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ that is not divisible by a monomial" (Abstract, l.9); "which are not divisible by a monomial" (l.9). In the body the same condition is always phrased via $t^\gamma$ (see A.10, J). Note: the paper never defines "monomial"; from context it means $t^\gamma$ with $\gamma < 0$ (my inference).

### A.4 Support `Supp(a)` — [DEF]

- "the support of $a$ is the set $\mathrm{Supp}(a) = \{\gamma \in G \mid a_\gamma \neq 0\}$" (§1, p.1277, l.13).
- Notation: roman `\mathrm{Supp}(a)` with capital S (45×); the word "support" 15×. Set-builder with a vertical bar `|` (rendered `\mid`).
- Typical phrases: "$0$ is a limit point of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$" (l.68, l.83, l.85), "$0$ is not in the closure of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$" (l.83), "$0$ is an isolated point of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$" (l.83), "$0$ is a limit point of support of $a$" (l.50, no article), "zero as a limit point of their support" (l.192, l.206), "the least elements of $\mathrm{Supp}(b)$ and $\mathrm{Supp}(c)$" (l.177), "$j$ has finite support" (l.173), "the limit points of the support of $a$" (l.37), "the closure of the support of $b$" (l.94).
- Support-set arithmetic in Prop. 3.2 (3)–(4): "$X(a) \subseteq \mathrm{Supp}(b) \cup \mathrm{Supp}(c)\backslash\{0\}$" (l.139); "$\mathrm{Supp}(b) \cup \mathrm{Supp}(c)\backslash X(a) \cup \{0\}$ is a finite subset of $\mathrm{Supp}(b) \cap \mathrm{Supp}(c)$" (l.141). **Flag:** as transcribed, these expressions have no parentheses; the intended reading is evidently $(\mathrm{Supp}(b) \cup \mathrm{Supp}(c)) \setminus (X(a) \cup \{0\})$ (cf. the proof at l.173: "if $x \notin X(a) \cup \{0\}$"). Whether the printed article has parentheses could not be verified. Set difference is written with a backslash (`\backslash`, 9×), never "$-$".

### A.5 Generalized power series — [DEF]

- Defining sentence: "Such a formal sum $a$ is called a *generalized power series* if $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$ is a well-ordered subset of $G$." (§1, p.1277, l.13). Abstract gloss: "the ring of generalized power series (i.e., formal sums with well-ordered support)" (l.9).
- Spelling: "generalized" (American, with z) throughout; "well-ordered" hyphenated 2× (l.9, l.13), "well ordered" unhyphenated 1× as a predicate ("and hence is well ordered", l.94).
- Count: "generalized power series" 10×. The objects are thereafter almost always called **elements** ("elements of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$", "an element of $R$"; "element(s) of" 30×), occasionally "generalized power series" as a count noun ("irreducibility criteria for generalized power series with real exponents", l.192; "the generalized power series $b$ and $c$", l.290). Never "series" alone except "Conway's series" / "the Conway series" (l.29, l.48) and "power series" in "ordinary power series" (l.13) and "the power series representing $a$" (l.62).
- Ring names: title "generalized power series rings"; abstract "the ring of generalized power series"; "rings of generalized power series" 3× (l.50, refs [6],[7]); "generalized power series rings" also at l.45 ("One motivation for studying generalized power series rings is the theory of surreal numbers").

### A.6 `K((G))` — [DEF] (notation; assertion of field property unsourced)

- "The collection of generalized power series with coefficients in $K$ and exponents in $G$ is denoted by $K((G))$ and can be shown to be a field with addition and multiplication defined as for ordinary power series." (§1, p.1277, l.13).
- Convention: double parentheses `K((G))`; "can be shown to be a field" with no citation (see C.1).

### A.7 `K((G^{≤0}))` — [DEF]

- "We define $K((G^{\leq 0}))$ to consist of those elements of $K((G))$ with only non-positive exponents, i.e., $a \in K((G))$ is in $K((G^{\leq 0}))$ if for all $\gamma \in \mathrm{Supp}(a)$ we have $\gamma \leq 0$. $K((G^{\leq 0}))$ is a non-noetherian integral domain, and we are interested in factorization properties of its elements." (§1, p.1277, l.13).
- Conventions: exponents are **non-positive** (≤ 0), so constants are included; the restriction is written as a superscript on the group inside the double parentheses, $K((G^{\leq 0}))$, $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$; "non-positive" hyphenated (l.9 "non-positive real exponents", l.13). "non-noetherian" lower-case n, hyphenated (1×).
- The strictly-negative variant appears only in the omnific-integer isomorphism: $\mathbb{R}((G^{<0})) \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ (l.48) and $\mathbb{R}((\mathbf{No}^{<0})) \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ (l.52); it is never named.

### A.8 `R = K((ℝ^{≤0}))` — [DEF] (notation)

- "Let $K$ be a field of characteristic zero and let $R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$. In this $K$-algebra, let $J$ denote ..." (§1, p.1278, l.33); "Now let $R$ denote the $K$-algebra $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$." (§2, p.1279, l.62).
- Both `R` and the explicit `K((ℝ^{≤0}))` are used throughout; the explicit form occurs 31×, and the equational form "$R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$" 13×, typically in the hypotheses of statements. Propositions 2.1–2.3 and Theorem 2.4 (Berarducci's results) are stated with the explicit $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ rather than $R$ ("Let $b, c \in R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$", l.74; "For $b, c \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$", l.87; "Let $0 \neq b \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$", l.94).
- What `R` is called: "$K$-algebra" (3×), "ring" ("In the ring $R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$", l.206; "Pulling back to the ring $R$", l.216; "the ring $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ contains prime elements", l.50), "integral domain" ("Since $R$ is an integral domain", l.270), "non-noetherian integral domain" (l.13). Never "domain" alone, never "valuation ring", never "Hahn series".

### A.9 Order type `ot(a)` — [DEF]

- "For $a \in K((G^{\leq 0}))$ we define the *order type* of $a$, $ot(a)$, to be the order type of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$. In general, $ot(a)$ is an ordinal number, and for the case when $G = \mathbb{R}$ it is a countable ordinal." (§1, p.1278, l.29).
- Notation: `ot(a)` (24×), lower-case, no backslash-operator visible in the transcription — **whether it is typeset italic or roman could not be verified.** Word form "order type" 33×; plural "order types" 8× ("the order types of $b$ and $c$ are $\omega$ or $\omega + 1$", l.122, l.145, l.151; "have finite order types", l.224, l.244).
- Characteristic phrasings: "$a$ has order type $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$" (l.29); "elements ... with order type $\omega^2$" (l.31, 5× "with order type"); "elements of order type $\omega^2$" (§4 title, l.206; "of order type" 4×); "whose order type is $\omega^2$" (l.106); "whose support has order type $\omega^2$" (l.9); "Assume that $ot(a)$ is $\omega^2$ or $\omega^2 + 1$" (standing hypothesis, l.33, l.108, l.124, l.132, l.194, l.200, l.208); "$c = \frac{1}{b}a$ has the same order type as $a$" (l.110); "whose order type is not a product of two smaller order types" (l.31); "if the order type of an element not in $J$ is not the product of two smaller ordinals" (l.100).
- Convention: the paper always treats $\omega^2$ and $\omega^2+1$ together (with or without a constant term), and $\omega$ and $\omega+1$ together; "order type $\omega^2$" in titles and prose stands for both.

### A.10 The ideal `J` and "divisible by $t^\gamma$" — [DEF]

- "In this $K$-algebra, let $J$ denote the ideal of all elements that are divisible by $t^\gamma$ for some $\gamma < 0$" (§1, p.1278, l.33); "Let $J$ denote the ideal of all elements of $R$ that are divisible by $t^\gamma$ for some $\gamma < 0$." (§2, p.1279, l.62).
- Complement phrasings for "not in J": "is not divisible by $t^\gamma$ for any $\gamma < 0$" (l.29); "not divisible by a monomial" (abstract, 2×); "do not have a trivial factor of $t^\gamma$ for some $\gamma \leq 0$" (l.31 — **flag:** "$\gamma \leq 0$" here, against "$\gamma < 0$" everywhere else; $\gamma = 0$ would be vacuous, so this is probably a slip in the original or transcription); "an element not in $J$" (l.100).
- The standing non-degeneracy hypothesis is written in three interchangeable ways: "$a \notin J + K$" (5×; Theorems A, B, and l.68, l.85), "$\widetilde{a} \neq 0$" (10×; all §3–4 statements), and in words "$0$ is a limit point of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$" / "have zero as a limit point of their support" (l.192, l.206, l.50). The paper asserts their equivalence: "Clearly, $\widetilde{a} \neq 0$ if and only if $0$ is a limit point of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$." (l.68).
- Membership of $J$ is used via "$C \in J + K$", "$C \in J$", "$B \in J$" (l.237), and elements of $J$ are named $j, j_1, j_2, j_3$: "$c = kb + j + k'$ where $j \in J$ and $k' \in K$" (l.173); "there exist $k_4, k_5, k_6 \in K$ and $j_1, j_2, j_3 \in J$ such that" (l.216). "$j$ has finite support" (l.173); "$j_1, j_2$ and $j_3$ have finite order types" (l.224).

### A.11 `\overline{R} = R/J`, the map `π`, and the germ of `a` at zero `\overline{a}` — [DEF]

- "The quotient $R/J$ will be denoted by $\overline{R}$. Note that $\overline{R}$ is an infinite-dimensional $K$-algebra. For every element $a \in R$, we define the *germ of $a$ at zero* to be the image of $a$ under the canonical homomorphism $\pi : R \to \overline{R}$. For $a \in R$, the germ of $a$ at zero will be denoted by $\overline{a} \in \overline{R}$." (§2, p.1279–80, l.62–66).
- Notation: overline for the image in $R/J$; applies to scalars too: "$k\overline{a} = \overline{ka} = \overline{k}\,\overline{a}$" (l.68); "$\overline{R}/\overline{K}$" (l.68); "$\overline{k}$", "$\overline{c_x}$", "$\overline{b_x}$", "$\overline{a_\nu}$" (l.85, l.162). Product of germs written by juxtaposition: "$\overline{b^{|\beta}}\ \overline{c^{|\xi}}$" (l.76, l.79).
- Motivation sentence: "For an element $a \in R$, we often need to focus on properties of the part of the power series representing $a$ with exponents just to the left of zero." (l.62).

### A.12 `\widetilde{R} = R/(J+K)`, the map `\widetilde{π}`, and the germ of `a` to the left of zero `\widetilde{a}` — [DEF]

- First definition: "define a $K$-vector space: $\widetilde{R} = R/(J + K)$." (§1, p.1278, l.33); "the image of this element under the canonical homomorphism $\widetilde{\pi} : R \to \widetilde{R}$" (l.33).
- Full statement: "Recall from the introduction that $\widetilde{R}$ denotes the $K$-vector space $R/(J+K) \cong \overline{R}/\overline{K}$, and for every element $a \in R$, $\widetilde{a} \in \widetilde{R}$ denotes the image of $a$ under the canonical homomorphism $\widetilde{\pi} : R \to \widetilde{R}$. $\widetilde{a}$ is the *germ of $a$ to the left of zero*. Clearly, $\widetilde{a} \neq 0$ if and only if $0$ is a limit point of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$." (§2, p.1280, l.68).
- Convention explicitly fixed: $\widetilde{R}$ is a **$K$-vector space**, not an algebra, and the paper warns about the non-multiplicativity of the tilde: "Also note that for $k \in K$, and $a \in R$, we have $k\overline{a} = \overline{ka} = \overline{k}\,\overline{a}$ while $\widetilde{k}\widetilde{a} = 0 \neq \widetilde{ka} = k\widetilde{a}$ as long as $a \notin J + K$ and $k \neq 0$." (l.68). Elsewhere $\widetilde{R}$ is "the vector space $V(a)$"'s ambient space: "As a subspace of $\widetilde{R}$" (l.143), "linearly dependent elements of $\widetilde{R}$" (l.143), "linearly independent in $\widetilde{R}$" (l.321).
- Count: $\widetilde{R}$ 13×, $\overline{R}$ 6×. The tilde dominates in §3–4; the overline is used only where multiplicativity (Prop. 2.1) is needed, and then the paper passes to the tilde: "So $\widetilde{a^{|x}} = c_x\widetilde{b} + b_x\widetilde{c}$." (l.166–168).
- Verbs for taking the image: "the image of $a$ under the canonical homomorphism" (l.33, l.62, l.68); "Moding out by $J + K$ and passing to $\widetilde{R}$ we have" (l.294 — spelling "Moding" as transcribed; possibly "Modding" in print, unverified); "passing to $\widetilde{R}$, we have" (l.317); reverse direction: "Pulling back to the ring $R$, there exist $k_4, k_5, k_6 \in K$ and $j_1, j_2, j_3 \in J$ such that" (l.216).

### A.13 Truncation `b_{|γ}` and translated truncation `b^{|γ}` — [REUSE from Berarducci, notation adopted; see B.1] (recorded here because the defining sentences are the paper's own)

- "Following Berarducci, if $b = \sum_\beta b_\beta t^\beta \in R$ and $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$, then we define $b_{|\gamma} = \sum_{\beta \leq \gamma} b_\beta t^\beta$ and $b^{|\gamma} = t^{-\gamma} b_{|\gamma}$. We call $b_{|\gamma}$ the *truncation of $b$ at $\gamma$*, and $b^{|\gamma}$ the *translated truncation of $b$ at $\gamma$*." (§2, p.1280, l.70).
- Earlier, in §1: "for $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$ we define the *translated truncation* of $a$ at $\gamma$ to be $a^{|\gamma} = t^{-\gamma} \sum_{\alpha \leq \gamma} a_\alpha t^\alpha$. The translated truncation of $a$ at $\gamma$ is an element of $R$" (§1, p.1278, l.33).
- Conventions: truncation is **inclusive** (sum over $\beta \leq \gamma$, so the term at $\gamma$ is kept); subscript `_{|γ}` for the truncation, superscript `^{|γ}` for the translated truncation; the translation multiplies by $t^{-\gamma}$ so that the truncation point moves to $0$. Count: "truncation" 4×, "translated truncation" 3×; the notation $b^{|\gamma}$, $a^{|x}$ etc. is pervasive (it is the only way the paper localises at a point); $b_{|\gamma}$ appears only in its definition.

### A.14 Germ of `b` at `γ` and germ of `b` to the left of `γ` — [DEF]

- "In addition, we call $\overline{b^{|\gamma}}$ the *germ of $b$ at $\gamma$*, and $\widetilde{b^{|\gamma}}$ the *germ of $b$ to the left of $\gamma$*." (§2, p.1280, l.70).
- Intro version: "the image of this element under the canonical homomorphism $\widetilde{\pi} : R \to \widetilde{R}$ will be called the *germ of $a$ to the left of $\gamma$* and will be denoted by $\widetilde{a^{|\gamma}}$. Note that the germ of $a$ to the left of $\gamma$ is zero unless $\gamma$ is a limit point of the support of $a$." (§1, p.1278, l.33).
- Usage: "$\overline{b^{|y}}$, the germ of $b$ at $y$, will be zero exactly when the ordinal value of $b$ at $y$ is zero" (l.151); "the germs of $a$ to the left of $n$ of the limit points of the support of $a$ are linearly independent" (l.37). Count: "germ" 9×; "to the left of" 6×; "at zero" 5×.
- Convention: "germ" is a word for a residue class in a quotient, with two flavours — "at" (mod $J$) and "to the left of" (mod $J+K$); "at zero"/"to the left of zero" when $\gamma = 0$.

### A.15 Ordinal value `v(a)`; ordinal value of `a` at `γ` — [REUSE from Berarducci, re-symbolled; see B.1]

- "One of the main tools used in this paper is the notion, introduced by Berarducci, of ordinal value for an element of $R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$. For $a \in R$, the *ordinal value* of $a$ (at zero) is an ordinal in $\mathbf{H}$, denoted by $v(a)$ (Berarducci [1] uses $v_J(a)$ and Pitteloud [6] uses $v_0(a)$), and defined by:" (§2, p.1280, l.81), followed by (l.83):
  $$v(a) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \overline{a} = 0, \text{ i.e., if } 0 \text{ is not in the closure of } \mathrm{Supp}(a), \\ 1 & \text{if } \overline{a} \neq 0 \text{ and } \widetilde{a} = 0, \text{ i.e., if } 0 \text{ is an isolated point of } \mathrm{Supp}(a), \\ \min\{ot(b) \mid b \in R \text{ and } \widetilde{b} = \widetilde{a}\} & \text{if } \widetilde{a} \neq 0, \text{ i.e., if } 0 \text{ is a limit point of } \mathrm{Supp}(a). \end{cases}$$
- Relation to order type (the paper's own gloss, unattributed): "If $a \notin J + K$ and $0$ is not in the support of $a$, then $ot(a)$ is a limit ordinal and $v(a)$ is precisely the principal part of $ot(a)$. On the other hand, if $a \notin J + K$ and $0 \in \mathrm{Supp}(a)$, then $ot(a) = \beta + 1$ for some limit ordinal $\beta$ and $v(a)$ is the principal part of $\beta$. For example, if $0$ is a limit point of $\mathrm{Supp}(a)$ and $ot(a) = \omega^2 \cdot 2 + \omega \cdot 5 + 1$, then $v(a) = \omega$." (l.85). Note the ordinal arithmetic convention in the example: $\omega^2 \cdot 2$ (ordinal times natural number on the right, with $\cdot$).
- Localised version: "For $a \in R$ and $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$ we call $v(a^{|\gamma})$ the *ordinal value of $a$ at $\gamma$*." (l.85). No separate symbol is introduced for it — always $v(a^{|\gamma})$, $v(b^{|y})$, $v(a^{|x})$.
- "Note that, for $a = \sum_\gamma a_\gamma t^\gamma \in R$ and $\nu \in \mathbb{R}$, $v(a^{|\nu}) = 1$ is equivalent to $\overline{a^{|\nu}} = \overline{a_\nu}$ where $a_\nu$ is a non-zero element of $K$." (l.85).
- Motivating sentence: "What makes the ordinal value valuable is the following multiplicative property:" (l.85), leading to Prop. 2.2.
- Phrasing: "the ordinal value of $a$ is greater than or equal to $\omega^2$ only at zero" (l.120); "the ordinal value of $b$ at $0$ is $1$" (l.122); "the ordinal value of each of these is $\omega$" (l.210); "the ordinal value of $b$ at $y$ and the ordinal value of $c$ at $z$ are at least $1$" (l.151). Count: "ordinal value" 12×.

### A.16 Critical point — [DEF (term's lineage unstated; the existence lemma is Berarducci's)]

- "Let $0 \neq b \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$. Then, by Lemma 10.1 of Berarducci [1], the set of ordinals $\{v(b^{|x}) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}\}$ has a maximum $\alpha$. In addition, the set $\{y \mid v(b^{|y}) \neq 0\}$ is contained in the closure of the support of $b$ and hence is well ordered. A number $x \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$ is called the *critical point* of $b$ if $x$ is the smallest real number such that $v(b^{|x}) = \alpha$." (§2, p.1281, l.94).
- The paper does not say whether the name "critical point" is Berarducci's; it follows "Lemma 10.1 of Berarducci" and precedes "Lemma 10.4 of Berarducci" (Prop. 2.3, stated in terms of critical points), so the reader is left to infer that the notion is Berarducci's. Record as: introduced in this paper's text with the definite article ("the critical point"), defined for non-zero $b$ only, uniquely determined (the *smallest* real attaining the maximum).
- Phrasing: "with critical points $x, y$ respectively" (Prop. 2.3, l.96); "Let $y$ and $z$ be critical points of $b$ and $c$ respectively" (l.116); "$0$ is the critical point of $b$" (l.122); "let the critical point of $B$ be at $y$ where $y \leq 0$" (l.244); "The critical point of $b$ is at zero" (l.244); "But this is impossible if $y$ is the critical point of $B$" (l.248). Count: "critical point(s)" 7×.

### A.22 Ordinal-theoretic notation: `OR`, `H`, additive principal, Cantor normal form, principal part — [REUSE: "We follow the notation and vocabulary of Berarducci [1] closely"; definitions given in the paper's own words]

- "Let $OR$ denote the class of all ordinal numbers and let $\mathbf{H} = \{\rho \in OR \mid \rho = 0 \text{ or } \exists\alpha \in OR \text{ with } \rho = \omega^\alpha\}$. Elements of $\mathbf{H}$ are called *additive principal* since they are exactly those ordinals that cannot be written as the ordinal sum of two strictly smaller ordinals." (§2, p.1279, l.56). Convention: $0 \in \mathbf{H}$ (so that $v(a) = 0$ is allowed); $1 = \omega^0 \in \mathbf{H}$.
- "It follows that for every ordinal number $\alpha \neq 0$, there are uniquely determined ordinals $\alpha_1 \geq \cdots \geq \alpha_n$ such that $\alpha = \omega^{\alpha_1} + \cdots + \omega^{\alpha_n}$. This is called the *Cantor normal form* of $\alpha$, and $\omega^{\alpha_n}$ is called the *principal part* of $\alpha$." (l.56). Conventions: Cantor normal form written with repeated terms and **no integer coefficients** (non-increasing exponents $\alpha_1 \geq \cdots \geq \alpha_n$); **"principal part" is the last (smallest) term $\omega^{\alpha_n}$**, not the leading term. Count: "Cantor normal form" 3×, "principal part" 3×, "additive principal" 1×.
- "ordinal number" 4× (formal), "ordinal" 33×; "limit ordinal" 2× (l.85); "countable ordinal" 1× (l.29).

### A.23 Natural sum `⊕` and natural product `⊙` — [REUSE (Berarducci's vocabulary) with the paper's own definitions]

- "Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be non-zero ordinals and consider their Cantor normal forms $\alpha = \omega^{\alpha_1} + \ldots + \omega^{\alpha_n}$ and $\beta = \omega^{\alpha_{n+1}} + \ldots + \omega^{\alpha_{n+m}}$. Let $\pi$ be defined as a permutation of integers $1, \ldots, n+m$ with the property that $\alpha_{\pi(1)} \geq \cdots \geq \alpha_{\pi(n+m)}$. Now the *natural sum* of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is denoted by $\alpha \oplus \beta$ and defined by $\alpha \oplus \beta = \omega^{\alpha_{\pi(1)}} + \cdots + \omega^{\alpha_{\pi(n+m)}}$. In addition for all ordinals $\alpha$, we set $0 \oplus \alpha = \alpha \oplus 0 = \alpha$." (§2, p.1279, l.58). (The letter $\pi$ is reused for the canonical homomorphism $R \to \overline{R}$ at l.62–66.)
- "The *natural product* of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is denoted by $\alpha \odot \beta$ and is first defined on $\mathbf{H}$ by $\omega^\alpha \odot \omega^\beta = \omega^{\alpha \oplus \beta}$ and $\gamma \odot 0 = 0 \odot \gamma = 0$. The definition of $\odot$ is then extended to all ordinals using the Cantor normal form and distributivity: $\gamma \odot (\alpha \oplus \beta) = (\alpha \oplus \beta) \odot \gamma = (\alpha \odot \gamma) \oplus (\beta \odot \gamma)$." (l.58).
- "Note that the natural sum and product are commutative, unlike the usual addition and multiplication of ordinals. In addition, it is easy to show (Lemma 3.5 of Berarducci [1]) that the natural sum and product are strictly increasing, i.e., if $\alpha < \beta$, then $\alpha \oplus \gamma < \beta \oplus \gamma$, and, provided $\gamma \neq 0$, $\alpha \odot \gamma < \beta \odot \gamma$." (l.60).
- Names "natural sum"/"natural product" (3× each; never "Hessenberg"); symbols $\oplus$ (12×, including the two direct-sum uses) and $\odot$ (21×). Ordinary ordinal product written with $\cdot$ ("$\omega^2 \cdot 2 + \omega \cdot 5 + 1$", l.85) or juxtaposed in exponents; ordinary ordinal sum with $+$ ("$\omega^2 + 1$").
- Invocation phrase: "the fact that the natural product is strictly increasing" (l.116); "Since the natural product is strictly increasing, the only possibility is if $v(B^{|y}) = 0$" (l.248).

## Part B. What the paper reuses, with attribution

### B.1 From Berarducci [1] (A. Berarducci, "Factorization in generalized power series", *Trans. AMS* 352(2):553–577, 2000)

Berarducci is the paper's primary source: "Berarducci" occurs 19×. The blanket statement of dependence:

- "We begin with a number of definitions related to ordinal numbers. We follow the notation and vocabulary of Berarducci [1] closely, and we refer the reader to Hrbacek and Jech [4, Chapter 7] for elementary definitions and facts about ordinals." (§2, p.1279, l.56). — Scope as written: the ordinal-theoretic material (OR, **H**, additive principal, Cantor normal form, principal part, natural sum/product). **Unchanged** except as noted.

Itemised borrowings:

1. **Existence of irreducibles (result).** "Not every integral domain has irreducible elements. However, Berarducci [1] recently proved that if $K$ is a field of characteristic zero and $G$ is an ordered abelian divisible group, then there exist irreducible elements in $K((G^{\leq 0}))$." (§1, p.1277, l.15). Reused as motivation, unchanged.

2. **Irreducibility by order type $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$ (result).** "When $K$ has characteristic zero, Berarducci [1] proved that if $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ is not divisible by $t^\gamma$ for any $\gamma < 0$, and if $a$ has order type $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$, then both $a$ and $a + 1$ are irreducible elements of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$. In particular, Conway's series $\sum_n t^{-1/n} + 1$ is irreducible." (§1, p.1278, l.29). Restated formally as **Theorem 2.4 (Theorem 10.5 of Berarducci [1])**: "Let $a \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ with $ot(a) = \omega^{\omega^\alpha}$ where $\alpha$ is an ordinal. If $\widetilde{a} \neq 0$, then $a$ and $a + 1$ are irreducible elements of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$." (§2, p.1281, l.102). Gloss: "Finally we have the main theorem of Berarducci that produces irreducible elements of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$. The theorem basically says that if the order type of an element not in $J$ is not the product of two smaller ordinals, then the element itself is irreducible." (l.100). Also: "Berarducci shows that elements whose order type is not a product of two smaller order types (and do not have a trivial factor of $t^\gamma$ for some $\gamma \leq 0$) must be irreducible." (l.31). Abstract version: "Berarducci (2000) constructed an irreducible omnific integer, in the sense of Conway (2001), by first proving that an element of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ that is not divisible by a monomial and whose support has order type $\omega$ (or $\omega^{\omega^\alpha}$ for some ordinal $\alpha$) must be irreducible." (l.9). Note the hypothesis is phrased three ways across these restatements (not divisible by $t^\gamma$; not in $J$; $\widetilde{a} \neq 0$).

3. **Extension to omnific integers (result).** "Berarducci [1] extended his irreducibility results appropriately and hence was able to provide the Conway series as an example of an irreducible omnific integer." (§1, p.1279, l.48).

4. **Truncation notation (notation), adopted unchanged.** "Following Berarducci, if $b = \sum_\beta b_\beta t^\beta \in R$ and $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$, then we define $b_{|\gamma} = \sum_{\beta \leq \gamma} b_\beta t^\beta$ and $b^{|\gamma} = t^{-\gamma} b_{|\gamma}$." (§2, p.1280, l.70). The paper's own names "truncation of $b$ at $\gamma$" / "translated truncation of $b$ at $\gamma$" are attached in the next sentence without saying whether the names are Berarducci's.

5. **Multiplicativity of germs (result).** "**Proposition 2.1** (Lemma 7.5 of Berarducci [1]). Let $b, c \in R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ and $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$. Then (1) there are only finitely many pairs $(\beta, \xi) \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0} \times \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}$ with $\beta + \xi = \gamma$ and $\overline{b^{|\beta}c^{|\xi}} \neq 0 \in \overline{R}$, (2) $\overline{(bc)^{|\gamma}} = \sum_{\beta+\xi=\gamma} \overline{b^{|\beta}}\ \overline{c^{|\xi}}$." (§2, p.1280, l.74–79). Introduced by "The following will be useful." (l.72).

6. **Ordinal value (notion), re-symbolled.** "One of the main tools used in this paper is the notion, introduced by Berarducci, of ordinal value for an element of $R = K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$. For $a \in R$, the *ordinal value* of $a$ (at zero) is an ordinal in $\mathbf{H}$, denoted by $v(a)$ (Berarducci [1] uses $v_J(a)$ and Pitteloud [6] uses $v_0(a)$)" (§2, p.1280, l.81). — The **name** "ordinal value" is credited to Berarducci and kept; the **symbol** is changed from Berarducci's $v_J(a)$ (and Pitteloud's $v_0(a)$) to the bare $v(a)$. No reason is given for dropping the subscript. The definition that follows (l.83) is stated in the paper's own terms (via $\overline{a}$, $\widetilde{a}$, and $\min\{ot(b) \mid \widetilde{b} = \widetilde{a}\}$) without saying whether it is Berarducci's definition or an equivalent reformulation.

7. **Properties of the ordinal value (result).** "**Proposition 2.2** (Lemma 5.5 and Theorem 9.7 of Berarducci [1]). For $b, c \in K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ we have (a) $v(b + c) \leq \max\{v(b), v(c)\}$ with equality if $v(b) \neq v(c)$, (b) $v(bc) = v(b) \odot v(c)$." (§2, p.1280, l.87–91). Introduced by "What makes the ordinal value valuable is the following multiplicative property:" (l.85).

8. **Existence of the maximum ordinal value / critical point (result).** "Then, by Lemma 10.1 of Berarducci [1], the set of ordinals $\{v(b^{|x}) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}\}$ has a maximum $\alpha$." (§2, p.1281, l.94). See A.16 for the unattributed naming of "critical point".

9. **Critical points multiply (result).** "**Proposition 2.3** (Lemma 10.4 of Berarducci [1]). Let $b$ and $c$ be non-zero elements of $K((\mathbb{R}^{\leq 0}))$ with critical points $x, y$ respectively. Then $v((bc)^{|x+y}) = v(b^{|x}) \odot v(c^{|y})$." (§2, p.1281, l.96–98).

10. **Natural sum/product strictly increasing (result).** "it is easy to show (Lemma 3.5 of Berarducci [1]) that the natural sum and product are strictly increasing" (§2, p.1279, l.60).

11. **Open problems (pointer).** "We refer the reader to Berarducci [1] for a list of open problems." (§1, p.1279, l.52).

12. **Ordinal vocabulary** (OR, **H**, "additive principal", Cantor normal form, "principal part", natural sum $\oplus$, natural product $\odot$): covered by the blanket "We follow the notation and vocabulary of Berarducci [1] closely" (l.56); the definitions are given in the paper's words (A.22–A.23). Whether any of these individual names or symbols differ from Berarducci's is not stated.

## Part F. Every passage where the paper disagrees with, corrects, or departs from another paper's usage

The paper contains **no explicit correction** of any other paper. The departures it records are:
